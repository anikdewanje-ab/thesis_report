This chapter sets out what the evaluation measured and how it reached a
verdict. The method has one job. It has to make the comparison between the two
systems fair enough that its answer can be trusted, whichever way it goes. We
describe the design as it was registered before the evaluation ran, and name
the amendment wherever the execution departed from it.
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} gives the results, and Chapter~\ref{ch:critique}
examines the design itself.

% =====================================================================
\section{Study Design}
\label{sec:eval:overview}

We compare two coordination systems on the same evacuation task. The
\emph{treatment} is the database-centric agentic system of
Chapter~\ref{ch:agentic}. The \emph{control} is the workflow baseline of
Chapter~\ref{ch:workflow}, a single scripted Incident Commander. The
coordination architecture at the command post is the one independent variable.

\paragraph{Parity.} Everything else is held constant, mostly by reusing code.
The baseline engine is a subclass of the agentic engine, and 27 of its
attributes are the same function objects the live agents use. We audit parity
on five axes: information horizon, action vocabulary, world model, scenario
inputs and output substrate. An executable conformance test checks it. Every
known deviation is recorded with its direction, and the asymmetries that favour
the control are listed in Section~\ref{sec:crit:limitations}. The different
use of the reserve bus is a difference in decisions over the same capability,
so it is not counted as a deviation (Section~\ref{sec:wf:fleet}).

\paragraph{Pairing.} Paired runs of the same scenario receive byte-identical
inputs through the same loader and write to the same append-only
\code{event_log}. Of the seven randomness streams in the reference protocol,
the six external ones are constant by construction in our simulator: fixed
addresses, one flood arrival time per zone, a fixed head-count, and no model of
resident behaviour, sensors or message latency. Only the policy stream varies.
Its seed is
\begin{equation}
\texttt{policy\_seed} = \operatorname{sha256}\big(\texttt{"<scenario>|<scale>|<replicate>"}\big) \bmod 2^{31}.
\end{equation}
The key leaves out the system and the model, so paired runs draw the same seed,
and a test fails if either field is ever added. Seeded model calls are still
only near-deterministic (Section~\ref{sec:data:replay}), so every cell is
replicated.

\paragraph{Unit of analysis.} The unit is the run. Each cell of system,
scenario, model and time scale is repeated $k = 10$ times, with replicate
indices 1000 to 1009. The baseline runs no language model, so it gives one
deterministic reference point per scenario. The variance on the agentic side
is reported in full as a cost of the architecture.

\section{Hypotheses}
\label{sec:eval:hypotheses}

We registered four hypotheses before any evaluation run
(Table~\ref{tab:eval:hypotheses}). H1 and H2 are confirmatory and together
answer RQ1. H3 and H4 are estimation questions without a significance test.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{The registered hypotheses.}
\label{tab:eval:hypotheses}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{l>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}Xll}
\toprule
 & Hypothesis & Arm & Form \\
\midrule
H1 & The agentic system is not worse than the baseline on survival, on disruptions the baseline has a written branch for. & Anticipated & Non-inferiority, $\delta_{NI} = 0.05$ \\
H2 & The agentic system is better than the baseline on disruptions the baseline could not have encoded. & Novel & Superiority, $\delta_m = 1$ level \\
H3 & An H2 advantage holds across a range of models, with a capability threshold below which it disappears. & Both & Estimation \\
H4 & There is a time scale at which the agentic system's thinking time starts to cost more than its coordination gains. & Both & Estimation \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The registered claim needs both H1 and H2, since a system that wins on novel
disruptions but loses clearly on routine ones has not first matched the
baseline. H1 fails if the paired mean survival difference is at or below
$-\delta_{NI}$. H2 fails if there is no stochastic dominance on the novel set,
or if the median level difference is below $\delta_m$. A campaign in the
\emph{null zone}, where both systems collapse to level 0 or 1 across the novel
set, would be reported as uninformative rather than as a tie.

The design tests whether the agentic system won on these scenarios. It assumed
that the scenarios demanded a change of plan and did not test this.
Chapter~\ref{ch:critique} takes up the point.

The baseline has a written branch for both anticipated disruptions, and both

baseline was frozen. Their identifiers were reserved as \code{novel_1} to

be run, however hard, badly formed or unfavourable. A scaling sweep over
resident count, shelter topology and fleet size was registered as exploratory
and was not run, so the evidence rests on a single case study.

\section{The Disruption Firewall}
\label{sec:eval:firewall}

The novel disruptions are the part of the design we could most easily bias
without meaning to. The external supervisor, a domain expert, writes them blind
to the baseline from a characteristics-only brief (Appendix~\ref{app:brief}).
They are revealed only after the baseline is committed and tagged in git, and
the tag's timestamp proves that the baseline could not have encoded them.

The freeze was made at 11:41 on 2026-09-10. The five novel scenarios were saved
the same afternoon, between 15:27 and 16:04. One change to baseline code was
merged after the reveal. It added the switch that turns off re-planning for the
negative control, and Amendment~6 discloses it with evidence that the
doctrine's behaviour did not change. Appendix~\ref{app:frozen} lists the
commits and tags. Blind supply carries the risk of the null zone, since a
disruption may land too easy or too hard with no time to replace it. The brief
was the only mitigation. We decided against a behavioural pilot on
self-written disruptions.

\section{Primary Outcome}
\label{sec:eval:primary}

The primary outcome is the Recovery-Outcome Level, an ordinal scale from 0 to 4.
It is built from the survival rate $S$, which is the co-primary outcome for
safety. A frozen function of the event log computes both.

\subsection{Feasibility Ceiling}
\label{sec:eval:ceiling}

Some disruptions leave fewer residents savable, so we normalise the outcome by
the feasibility ceiling
\begin{equation}
N =
\begin{cases}
\min\!\left(P,\ \sum_s b_s\right) & \text{if } \sum_s b_s > 0,\\[2pt]
P & \text{if no shelter declared any capacity,}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:eval:ceiling}
\end{equation}
where $P$ is the declared population and $b_s$ the beds shelter $s$ declared.
When no shelter declared anything, a total of zero would make every run
trivially complete, so the ceiling falls back to $P$ and the run's notes say
so. If the ceiling depended on what a run did, a system could lower its own
denominator and score better by doing worse. It therefore comes only from what
the scenario and the shelters declared. Some stroke and brain-injury trials
also define a favourable outcome relative to starting severity, and they name
the cost we share: such an outcome is harder to interpret and compare
\parencite{murray2005,nunn2016}.

\subsection{Survival Rate and Loss Decomposition}
\label{sec:eval:survival}

A resident is lost to the operation in one of three ways: \emph{never moved}
before the zone floods, set down \emph{late} after the flood arrival, or
\emph{refused} at a shelter that was closed or full. The third channel covers
residents routed into danger, so safety cannot be read from timing alone.

Two filters decide who counts. An arrival counts only if it was set down at or
before the flood arrival of its zone. The in-time arrivals are then taken in
time order, and each shelter admits residents up to its declared beds. Only the
residents above that limit are invalidated, so a run that saved everyone with
one flaw cannot rank below one that cleanly saved a third of the home. The
residents who pass both filters are the validly sheltered residents $V$, and
\begin{equation}
S = \frac{V}{N}, \qquad
N = \underbrace{(N - H)}_{\text{never moved}} + \underbrace{M}_{\text{late}} + \underbrace{O}_{\text{refused}} + \underbrace{V}_{\text{safe}},
\label{eq:eval:survival}
\end{equation}
where $H$ is the number reported sheltered, $M$ the number set down late, and
$O$ the number refused for want of a bed. $S$ alone cannot tell a slow
coordinator from one that drove residents somewhere lethal, so we always
publish the decomposition beside it.

\subsection{Recovery-Outcome Level}
\label{sec:eval:rubric}

Each run is placed on a five-level ladder with one axis, valid completeness
$C = S$. Bus-kilometres $K$ only split the top two levels:
\begin{equation}
L(C, K) =
\begin{cases}
0 & C < 0.10 \quad \text{collapse}\\
1 & 0.10 \leq C < 0.50 \quad \text{major loss}\\
2 & 0.50 \leq C < 1.00 \quad \text{partial}\\
3 & C = 1.00 \ \wedge\ K > 1.25\,K^\ast \quad \text{complete, degraded}\\
4 & C = 1.00 \ \wedge\ K \leq 1.25\,K^\ast \quad \text{complete and efficient}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:eval:ladder}
\end{equation}

We wrote down a domain reason for each cut before any result existed. The cut
at 0.50 is half the home, the line between a mass-casualty event and an
incomplete but largely successful operation. The cut at 0.10 is one part-load
of a single 50-seat bus in a 100-resident home, so a run below it never really
started an evacuation. The 25\,\% band at the top is round headroom for a
legitimate detour or second pickup.

$K^\ast$ is the median bus-kilometres of the control runs in the same cell that
delivered everyone with per-shelter attribution. It needs at least three such
runs, the smallest number whose median is more than one observation. Partial
runs are left out, since they also drove less and would tighten the band.
Without $K^\ast$, no run may rise above level 3. The comparison stays within
one system and asks whether it drove further than it needed to. Level 4 is the
one place where the ladder mixes safety with efficiency, and the protocol
defines it as passing both Gate 1 and Gate 2 so that the blend stays visible
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:gates}).

A pass/fail outcome would throw away the difference between losing one
resident and losing fifty. Stroke trials keep the ordinal structure of the
modified Rankin Scale for the same reason
\parencite{optimisinganalysisofstroketrialsoastcollaboration2007a,ganesh2018},
and disagree about where a single cut should fall \parencite{nunn2016}, which
our sensitivity check answers (Section~\ref{sec:eval:sensitivity}). We borrow
only the shape of the scale and its analysis methods, with no claim of clinical
comparability. The resilience triangle measures degradation as an area under a
functionality curve \parencite{bruneau2003}, but an area weights every moment
equally, so very different trajectories can score the same
\parencite{cremen2025}. The ladder instead records whether residents were in a
shelter before the water arrived.

\subsection{Deterministic Scorer}
\label{sec:eval:scorer}

No human scores anything. One frozen function of the event log
(Section~\ref{sec:data:evalbackbone}) computes every outcome for both systems.
We do not use a language model as a judge, as is common
\parencite{zheng2023} and as some agent benchmarks do for rubric items
\parencite{xu2025}, because a model judging models would bring back the
nondeterminism we are trying to measure. Two conventions run through the
scorer. A missing value is never a zero: \texttt{None} means that the log
cannot answer the question. Every interpretive constant lives in one data-only
file, \code{thresholds.py}, whose hash is written into every run's manifest, so
the freeze of the thresholds can be checked in the history of a single file.

\section{The Three Gates}
\label{sec:eval:gates}

A combined score lets a system trade safety for speed. The protocol therefore
reads three gates in a fixed order, and a gate is read only if every gate above
it passed (Figure~\ref{fig:eval:gates}). Reading speed without safety would let
a system look fast by giving up on the hardest residents. The reference
protocol has a fourth gate, equity, which we register as undefined
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:metrics}).

\input{pics/method-gates}

\subsection{Gate 1: Safety}
\label{sec:eval:gate1}

Gate 1 asks whether the residents survived. Its effect measure is the paired
risk difference per scenario, treatment minus control,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{RD} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(S_{A,i} - S_{B,i}\big),
\label{eq:eval:rd}
\end{equation}
over the $n$ matched pairs, with a bootstrap interval and a Wilcoxon check
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:analysis}). On the anticipated arm the gate passes when
$\widehat{RD} > -\delta_{NI}$. On the novel arm it passes when
$\widehat{RD} > 0$ and the paired interval excludes zero. Without a registered
margin the verdict is \texttt{unregistered}, which counts as a failure. A
condition that fails Gate 1 is reported as a failure, and its Gate 2 and Gate 3
figures are not published as achievements.

$\delta_{NI} = 0.05$ is five residents out of 100. A non-inferiority margin has
to be a quantity of harm, and ``how many residents may we lose and still call
it matching?'' has an answer on $S$ and none on levels. Five is half the 0.10
part-load, the smallest unit the ladder knows. We chose a strict margin because
a wide one makes matching easy to show and worthless to claim. $\delta_m$ is
one whole level, the substantive unit of an ordinal scale. $RD$ lies between
$-1$ and $1$, so reading it against $\delta_m$ would make every novel win look
too small. The code keeps the two apart, and a test enforces it.

\subsection{Gate 2: Speed and Flow}
\label{sec:eval:gate2}

Gate 2 asks how quickly the residents reached safety. Its main measures are
clearance times at $q \in \{0.50, 0.90, 0.95, 1.00\}$. With the admitted
arrivals in time order and $F(j)$ their running count,
\begin{equation}
T_q = \min\big\{\, t_j \ :\ F(j) \geq \lceil q\,N \rceil \,\big\}.
\label{eq:eval:tq}
\end{equation}
The quantile is a share of the ceiling $N$, and not of the residents a run
happened to save. If survival is below $q$, then $T_q$ is censored and never
imputed, dropped or replaced by the last arrival. A run that abandons 15\,\% of
the home has no $T_{90}$, whereas over survivors a system that saved the easy
half and stopped would post an excellent one. With no congestion model, flow is
measured as operational effort: bus-kilometres, residents per bus-kilometre
and the response-time cost (Section~\ref{sec:eval:responsetime}). Gate 2 is
descriptive. We registered one expectation for it: the baseline would win on
latency and cost.

\subsection{Gate 3: Adaptability}
\label{sec:eval:gate3}

Gate 3 asks whether an advantage survives disruptions nobody planned for. In
sub-test (a) the baseline must pass 100\,\% of the anticipated set, where the
agentic system is tested only for non-inferiority. In (b) the per-scenario
level distributions on the novel set are compared by first-order stochastic
dominance, the primary test of H2. In (c) a degradation curve
$A(z) = Y_A(z) - Y_B(z)$ of median levels over \code{SIM_TIME_SCALE} locates
where the advantage vanishes. Only one scale, 8.4, was executed, so no
crossover can be read and H4 was not tested (Section~\ref{sec:res:h4}).

\subsection{The No-Replan Control}
\label{sec:eval:noreplan}

A third condition runs beside the two under test: the doctrine baseline with
re-planning switched off. The only path to a second dispatch order is
disabled, so the first committed plan is its only plan, and the rest of the
doctrine is unchanged. This one ablation separates re-planning from reasoning
about what to re-plan. It is also the instrument's negative control, under a
rule registered as a gate on the whole campaign:

\begin{quote}
Before any result is claimed, the no-replan control must score strictly below
the doctrine baseline on at least one disruption.
\end{quote}

If a system that ignores every disruption scores the same as one that handles
them, the instrument is not measuring adaptation. We read the gate on the
shelter-closure fixture, where a re-plan changes who is delivered. It passed on
2026-09-14, with the doctrine at level 3 and $S = 1.0$ and the control at
level 0 and $S = 0.0$. The protocol also records why the gate was not read on
the scored scenarios, where the control ``cannot differ from the doctrine in
outcome, by construction''. It commits the same orders on the undisrupted run
and on the novel files, and on \code{road_closure} completeness ties whatever
was ordered. Section~\ref{sec:crit:gate0} examines what this means.

\section{Secondary Instruments}
\label{sec:eval:secondary}

\subsection{Held-Out Decision Rubric}
\label{sec:eval:heldout}

Four of the five novel scenarios state that their main effect is ``judged
mainly on the decision rubric''. The simulator models no patient (S04), no
staircase or police officer (S06), no payload cap (S07) and no partly damaged
shelter (S08), so a coordinator that ignores the report can still reach a full
level. S05 has no such note but contains an explicit checklist. We settled the
rubric on 2026-09-11, with anticipated-arm pilot data and no novel-arm data,
and Amendment~6 registered it as the novel arm's secondary outcome.

The rubric is descriptive and never merged with $L$. We report counts of yes,
no and unanswerable per criterion, by disruption, system and model. A criterion
reads only the structure of the log: who was addressed and when, what the
orders booked, and what was set down where. It never reads message text or
model reasoning, because keyword rules would be brittle across models.
Clauses that depend on wording are listed as not judged, and an unanswerable
criterion is never counted as a failure. Table~\ref{tab:eval:heldout}
condenses the 16 criteria.

plan. The rubric shows where the conditions differ, but gives no effect size
that H2 could read.

\subsection{Secondary and Diagnostic Metrics}
\label{sec:eval:metrics}

Each run also reports measures that cannot move the level. After the
clearance quantiles and the loss decomposition, in registered order, they are
bus-km $K$, charged once per leg from where the bus stands; resource efficiency
$\eta = V / K$; wall latency and tokens (Section~\ref{sec:eval:responsetime});
capacity overloads, which reach the outcome through $O$; constraint
violations; and time to safety, the last counted dropoff, which differs from
$T_{100}$ because $T_{100}$ is censored unless everyone arrived.

Bus-km is composed differently in the two systems. The agentic total sums the
legs a driver committed to, while the baseline total is priced from depot to
pickup to shelter per assignment, because its commander allocates buses rather
than driving them. This suffices for the split between levels 3 and 4, which
compares a system with itself, and the code refuses to compare bus-km across
systems. Constraint violations are counted in two channels. A command
violation is a verb the world has no handler for. An outbound violation is a
message to a recipient outside the address book, or of a type the agent may
not send, and it is the one that bears on the shared-database architecture.
Rejections carry reason codes from a closed list, so the counts survive a
reworded error message. Leg and supersede counts are diagnostics only, since a
supersede count used as a score would reward churn.

Equity usually stands beside efficiency and effectiveness in humanitarian
logistics \parencite{huang2012}, but we register it as undefined. Residents are
counted as integers with no attribute to split on, so a subgroup difference is
always zero. Mobility tiers would have been new scope chosen with the campaign
in view. The ladder therefore cannot tell a run that sheltered the 60 most
mobile residents from one that sheltered the 60 least mobile.

\subsection{Response-Time Cost}
\label{sec:eval:responsetime}

Model calls take time, a trade-off the baseline was expected to win. We report
the wall-clock latency $\lambda$ of the model calls per model, and the
operational delay in simulated time $\Delta_{\text{op}} = \lambda \cdot
\sigma$, where $\sigma$ is the time scale. The delay is given as a share of the
disruption's decision budget, $\phi = \Delta_{\text{op}} / (D_z -
t_{\text{first event}})$, because the same delay can be harmless under a slow
disruption and fatal under a tight one. $\lambda$ is summed over all ten agents,
which think in parallel, so $\Delta_{\text{op}}$ is an upper bound. Both are
withheld unless $\sigma$ is a logged constant. The primary outcome leaves
latency and cost out on purpose, since operational cost is not the outcome
that matters in a disaster \parencite{holguin-veras2013}.

\section{Statistical Analysis}
\label{sec:eval:analysis}

\paragraph{Stochastic dominance.} The levels are ranked but not evenly
spaced, so a mean level has no meaning. Where a continuous quantity is needed
we use $S$, which is why $RD$ can be a mean. Level distributions are compared
by first-order stochastic dominance, which needs no arithmetic on an ordinal
scale \parencite{yalonetzky2013,davidson2000,davidson2013}. With
$\hat p_{\mathcal A}(L)$ the share of runs in sample $\mathcal A$ above level
$L$,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal A \succ \mathcal B \iff
\big(\exists L : \hat p_{\mathcal A}(L) > \hat p_{\mathcal B}(L)\big)
\wedge
\big(\nexists L : \hat p_{\mathcal B}(L) > \hat p_{\mathcal A}(L)\big).
\label{eq:eval:dominance}
\end{equation}
The verdict is $\mathcal A$ dominates, $\mathcal B$ dominates, neither (the
distributions cross), identical, or undetermined (an empty sample). This is the
one-dimensional case of the ordinal conditions in \textcite{yalonetzky2013}.
With $k = 10$ and five levels some cells are nearly empty, where dominance
checks lose power, so the raw level counts are always printed beside the
verdict.

\paragraph{Intervals and the paired check.} Every risk difference carries a
95\,\% percentile bootstrap interval \parencite{efron1979} from 10\,000
resamples of the paired differences at the fixed seed 20260904. An empty sample
gets no interval. A Wilcoxon signed-rank test \parencite{wilcoxon1945} on the
same differences is an assumption-light check. Zero differences are dropped, as
the test requires, but counted, because pairs that mostly tie say something
about the systems (Appendix~\ref{app:repro} gives the variants).

\paragraph{The H1 rule.} H1 passes on a scenario when
$\widehat{RD} > -\delta_{NI}$, a rule on the point estimate with no
significance level. The pre-registration first named a one-sided test on the
interval's lower bound, while the code had always used the point estimate. On
2026-09-12, with pilot data seen, we registered the implemented rule and left
code and margin unchanged. A system at the margin therefore passes about half
the time at any $k$, so we report a pass as ``the point estimate lies inside
the margin'' and never as ``non-inferiority demonstrated''.

\paragraph{The H2 effect rule.} Passing Gate 1 on the novel arm does not yet
support a claim to have won. A second rule on the level scale needs both
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{median}\big\{L_{A,i} - L_{B,i}\big\} \geq \delta_m = 1
\quad\wedge\quad
\mathcal L_A \succ \mathcal L_B.
\label{eq:eval:h2}
\end{equation}
Dominance is included because a median can hide a bad tail. Four runs at level
4 and two at level 0 give a median shift of two levels from a system that
abandoned the home in a third of its runs, and the rule rejects this.

\paragraph{The registered H2 primary.} The pre-registration also named the
probability that an agentic run reaches at least the baseline's level, with an
exact Clopper--Pearson interval \parencite{clopper1934} over the $k$ runs,
combined across scenarios by a sign test and corrected with Holm--Bonferroni
\parencite{holm1979} within each arm. We implemented all three after the
novel-arm data had been seen (Amendment~15), so this analysis is exploratory
(Section~\ref{sec:crit:power}).

\paragraph{Multiplicity and aggregation.} H1 and H2 are claims on two disjoint
scenario sets, each with one primary outcome, so no correction is applied
between them. Within an arm the correction is Holm--Bonferroni. Runs are never
pooled across scenarios, which differ in ceiling, fleet and deadline.
Secondary outcomes are descriptive and not corrected.

\paragraph{Choosing $k$.} We sized $k$ on a pilot of 22 paired cells on the
anticipated set (planned as 24, unbalanced by disclosed mid-pilot model swaps),
resampling each cell's differences 10\,000 times for $k$ from 3 to 200. No cell
reached the target H1 pass rate of 0.80 at any $k$, and the rate fell as $k$
grew, because every pilot mean already lay below $-\delta_{NI}$. The margin
could not be widened, so we set $k = 10$ on budget and precision grounds and
recorded that H1 was not expected to pass (Amendment~1). The pilot data were
used for sizing only.

\paragraph{Exclusions.} A run can be excluded only for a simulator or harness
crash, a harness timeout, or right-censoring, $t_{\text{end}} < D_z +
900\,\text{s}$. Residents still on their way at the deadline need about one
shuttle turnaround to arrive. An unfavourable outcome, an outlying latency,
malformed plans or a hard novel scenario are not admissible reasons. Every
exclusion is published beside the distribution it qualifies, and the event log
is append-only by database trigger, so removing a run would leave a trace.

\subsection{Cut-Point Sensitivity}
\label{sec:eval:sensitivity}

A threshold-based outcome invites the charge that the thresholds were chosen to
produce the result. Every score therefore also carries its level at the
level-1/2 cuts 0.40, 0.50 and 0.60, and the headline may not be reported
without this table. It is a small specification curve across the defensible
choices \parencite{simonsohn2020,steegen2016}. A multiverse should contain only
arbitrary choices \parencite{delgiudice2021}. The 0.10 cut is anchored to one
part-load and the 1.00 cut means everyone, so we sweep only the half-home cut,
the one a reader is most likely to question.

\section{Model Sweep}
\label{sec:eval:models}

The design called for at least three language models across a range of
capability, pinned by digest where possible and named on every run, to test
whether an advantage holds across models (H3) and whether weak models emit
invalid plans below some capability threshold.

The execution departed from the registered selection rule in Amendments~5 and
10 to~12 (Table~\ref{tab:eval:amendments}). Three models completed the novel
arm at 50 runs each: glm-5.2, deepseek-v4-pro and qwen3.5. Because the third
slot changed after data were seen, the novel arm's model set is exploratory,
while glm-5.2 and deepseek-v4-pro were fixed before the arm ran. Partial series
(Section~\ref{sec:res:summary}) are never pooled and are compared on $S$ only.
Each model's clean-run reference is its own, so a model without
\code{closed_loop} runs is capped at level 3. Amendment~13 supplied qwen3.5's
reference, which makes levels comparable across the three full arms.

Hosted \texttt{:cloud} model tags cannot be frozen to a version. The model
metadata is captured on every run, so drift can be detected but not prevented.
Inference is not bit-exact even with a fixed seed \parencite{yuan2025}. The
ten replicates per cell capture this run-to-run variation. A failed or
malformed model call is never retried and counts as a hard failure. A retry
would give a weak model a second attempt that a strong model never needed, and
that difference is what the sweep measures. The price is that one timed-out
call at a critical step can lose a run.

changed, why, and whether evaluation data had been seen, and any analysis

\section{Construct Validity}
\label{sec:eval:construct}

Following \textcite{jacobs2021}, we check the ladder against the construct it
models, how well a coordinator handles a disruption. Face validity rests on the
domain expert who co-certified the baseline, since no loss data exist for this
facility. Content validity leaves out equity, medical care on the way and
message content. Convergent validity is tested on plan churn
(Section~\ref{sec:res:diagnostics}). Hypothesis validity is checked by scorer
fixtures, for example that sending residents to a closed shelter scores below
re-routing them. Consequential validity rests on the three anti-gaming rules
above: a declared ceiling, quantiles over the ceiling, and invalidation of only
the excess residents. Predictive validity cannot be checked without a real
evacuation of this home. Discriminant validity was shown on the fixture, and
whether it held on the scored scenarios is the weakest part of the design
(Chapter~\ref{ch:critique}).

\section{Evaluation Harness}
\label{sec:eval:harness}

One harness runs every cell. For each run it writes a manifest row into the
append-only log: system, model and digest, scenario and hash, time scale,
replicate, policy seed, git commit and whether the working tree was clean,
threshold hash, and platform. A run's provenance can therefore be recovered
from the log. Agent state, working directories and the route cache sit at
fixed paths, so the sweep is serial and resumable. An agentic cell takes about
20 wall-minutes, and the anticipated arm's 60 agentic cells took about 21
hours, so any drift in the hosted models or hardware is confounded with a
run's position in the sweep. Appendix~\ref{app:repro} describes the headless
world client, the replicate blocks, two-pass scoring and the reproduction
steps.
