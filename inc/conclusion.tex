\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{ch:conclusion}

In this thesis we designed a database-centric agentic architecture for the
evacuation of a nursing home before a storm surge. Next to it we built a
workflow baseline that follows the DV~100 doctrine \parencite{fwdv100}. Both systems ran seven
scenarios, wrote to one shared append-only event log and were scored by one
deterministic scorer. We set the work up as a design-and-evaluation study.
The aim was to learn what such an architecture can do on a scored task, and to
report the answer in whichever direction it fell.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:concl:summary}

\paragraph{RQ1: Can the agentic architecture beat the scripted baseline?}
On our scenarios it could not. Both pre-registered hypotheses failed. Gate~1,
the test on resident survival, failed in 26 of 27 cells. The baseline
sheltered every resident in all 50 novel runs. The best agentic configuration
reached a capped mean survival of 0.894 (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}). The
agentic system also lost to a null policy that ignores every disruption. Its
regret against that policy was negative in all 15 novel cells, and 68 of 150
runs ended worse than doing nothing (Chapter~\ref{ch:critique}). The answer
holds for these scenarios only. No scenario in this thesis required
adaptation, because a controller that ignores the disruption already reaches
the top score (Section~\ref{sec:crit:gate0}). The baseline's perfect score is
therefore the null policy's score, and it says nothing about adaptability. We
answer RQ1 as ``did the agentic architecture win here''. Whether it can ever
win remains open.

\paragraph{RQ2: Why did it lose?}
The agents read the disruptions correctly, and the coordination between them
worked. Of 1,932 addressed requests, 93.8\% ended in a closed handshake. Most
of the lost residents were never dispatched at all, so the loss comes in whole
bus-loads. Of the 150 novel runs, 102 delivered everyone, 19 delivered exactly
50 and 18 delivered exactly 90 (Section~\ref{sec:res:loss}). The more often a
run revised its plan, the less it delivered. Full delivery fell from 90\% at
the minimum number of plan revisions to 32\% at six or more, and the number of
revisions correlates with survival at $r = -0.408$. This is why we speak of a
failure of commitment rather than of comprehension
(Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}). The link is a strong association. Its causal
direction stays open, since a run that is already failing may re-plan as a
result (Section~\ref{sec:crit:mechanism}).

\paragraph{RQ3: What must change, and what should a designer consider?}
Chapter~\ref{ch:design} answers with four groups of considerations, each tied
to a measurement in this study. The agent architecture should keep a leg
committed once it is on the road and confirm every delivery. The scenario
design should let a disruption change the world that is scored. The
evaluation should pass Gate~0 and be sized before the first run. The cost of
the agentic approach should be planned from the start.

\paragraph{The engineering questions.} The four supporting questions are
answered in the chapters that describe what we built. The actors, states,
events and dependencies of the evacuation are modelled in one PostgreSQL
schema (Chapter~\ref{ch:data}). Agents from different organizations are
integrated through one asynchronous engine, and the database also acts as the
broker that wakes them (Chapter~\ref{ch:agentic}). The append-only event log
serves runtime control and evaluation at the same time
(Sections~\ref{sec:data:eventlog} and~\ref{sec:data:evalbackbone}). The
comparison of the two systems is set out in Chapter~\ref{ch:eval} and
reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\section{Contributions Revisited}
\label{sec:concl:contributions}

The introduction promised four contributions
(Section~\ref{sec:intro:contributions}). Here we say what each one offers
beyond this study.

\paragraph{An agentic architecture and its baseline.} The two systems form a
matched pair. They differ only in how decisions are made, so a later study can
change one side and keep the other fixed (Chapters~\ref{ch:agentic},
\ref{ch:workflow} and~\ref{ch:data}). The agentic side shows one way to let
agents of different organizations coordinate through a database that holds
all state and wakes each agent when it has work.

\paragraph{A scored comparison with a negative result.} The comparison is
pre-registered and deterministic, so its answer can be checked and reproduced
from the stored runs. Earlier work with LLM agents in evacuation models the
people who evacuate or supports practitioners in planning
\parencite{sultimov2026,lee2025,li2026}. To our knowledge ours is the first head-to-head comparison
of the two approaches on a common evacuation case. It also reports decision
quality next to survival, and the two measures do not tell the same story
(Section~\ref{sec:res:quality}).

\paragraph{A diagnosis of the loss.} The diagnosis gives a future builder
something to act on. It points to one place in the architecture, the step
where a new message may cancel a leg that is already moving. It also warns
against reading healthy messaging as a sign of success.

\paragraph{A method for a fair comparison.} The method can be reused without
our architecture. A shared append-only log keeps the comparison fair, and new
measures can be computed from it long after the runs have ended. The regret
against a null policy shows whether a system does better than doing nothing. Gate~0 tests a scenario before any campaign is spent on it. Our own
novel arm fails this gate, and that failure is how we learned the gate is
needed.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:concl:future}

We rank the follow-up work by how much each item would change what we know.

\paragraph{1. A commitment-constrained variant.} The next experiment is a
version of the agentic system with a commitment boundary. It caps the number
of re-plans, or it forbids cancelling a leg that is already in flight. It
should run on the same scenarios under the same models. This needs one
campaign and no new scenarios. It is also the experiment that would settle
the causal direction that this thesis leaves open. If constrained runs deliver
more residents, plan churn is a cause of the loss. If they deliver the same,
churn is a symptom of runs that were already failing.

\paragraph{2. Scenarios that pass Gate~0.} A test of adaptability needs a
disruption that punishes a plan which ignores it. In our scenarios every
disruption arrived as a message and left the declared world unchanged. A
future scenario should change the state the scorer reads. When a shelter
reports fewer beds, it should really hold fewer people, and a no-replan
control should then lose residents. Only on such scenarios can the question
behind RQ1, whether an agentic architecture can ever beat a scripted one, be
answered.

\paragraph{3. An evaluation that can detect an effect.} With $k = 10$
replicates over five scenarios, the registered sign test could not return a
$p$-value below 0.0625, whatever the data (Section~\ref{sec:crit:power}). A
follow-up should size $k$ for the smallest effect it wants to detect. It
should also run more than one simulation time scale, because hypothesis~H4
was not tested at the single \code{SIM_TIME_SCALE} of 8.4. The model set
should be fixed and its versions pinned before the first run.

\paragraph{4. A broader case.} In our case the residents are a passive count
of 100. Modelling them individually, for example with different mobility
needs \parencite{alqahtani2025a}, would make it possible to ask about equity, which this study had to
leave undefined. Beyond that, the architecture and the method should be
tried on more facilities, other cities and other hazards. Only then can the
design considerations in Chapter~\ref{ch:design} be tested beyond the one case
they come from.

\section{Closing Remarks}
\label{sec:concl:closing}

We expected LLM agents to handle disruptions that a script did not foresee.
On the scenarios we had, the script delivered every resident and the agents
did not, although they read the situation correctly. This result does not
tell us how the agents would do where adaptation is really required. The
first two items of the future work, a commitment boundary and scenarios that
pass Gate~0, are the most direct way to find out.
