\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:intro:motivation}

Hamburg lies on the Elbe, upstream of the North Sea. A strong storm surge can
push water up the estuary and into the city. When a surge is forecast, people
who live near the river have to leave in time. For most of them this means a
car or a train. For the residents of a nursing home it means a planned
operation. Many of them cannot walk far, some need medical care on the way, and
all of them depend on vehicles and staff that the home does not own. Studies of
coastal evacuation show that moving elderly people is the part that dominates
the logistics \parencite{yin2024}. Models that ignore differences in mobility
underestimate how long the operation takes \parencite{alqahtani2025a}.

Such an evacuation is a coordination problem across organizations. A crisis
team decides. A transport company supplies buses and drivers. The police escort
the convoy, a medical service travels with it, and schools open as shelters.
Each organization sees only its own part of the situation, and all of them work
against one fixed deadline, the arrival of the water. The plan also rarely stays
as it was. A road closes, a shelter reports fewer beds, or a resident collapses
on a bus that is already moving.

In Germany such operations follow a written doctrine, DV~100
\parencite{fwdv100}. A computer simulation in this tradition is a script. It
encodes the steps of the doctrine and adds a branch for each disruption its
author expected. Research on business processes calls these anticipated
exceptions. It separates them from unanticipated exceptions, which a fixed
script cannot handle by itself \parencite{reichert2012}. \textcite{woods2015a}
draws a similar line between robustness against disruptions that were modelled
in advance and graceful extensibility beyond them. A script can be robust, but
it only covers the cases its author wrote down.

Agents built from large language models (LLMs) suggest a different design. An
LLM agent observes, reasons and acts in a loop, and it can revise its plan when
something unexpected happens \parencite{yao2023}. If each organization is
played by such an agent, and the agents coordinate through a shared database,
the system could in principle respond to a disruption that nobody scripted.
The critical literature on LLM planning gives reasons for
doubt \parencite{cemri2025}. Whether the promise holds when the score is the
number of residents who reach a shelter was an open question
(Section~\ref{sec:bg:related}).

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:intro:problem}

We study this question on one concrete case. A storm surge is forecast for
Hamburg. The Augustinum, a senior residence on the Elbchaussee near the river,
has to move its 100 residents to two schools that serve as shelters before the
water arrives. Each school offers 60 beds. The local transport company provides
two buses with 50 seats each, one as the primary bus and one in reserve. No bus
can carry everyone in one trip, and no shelter can take everyone, so the
allocation is a real decision. The resources belong to eight different
organizations. During the operation a disruption arrives that may force the
plan to change. Chapter~\ref{ch:casestudy} describes the case in detail.

We treat the problem as a design-and-evaluation study, in the sense of design
science research, which builds an artifact and then evaluates it
\parencite{hevner2004}. First, we design a
database-centric agentic architecture. Ten agents, each representing a person
at one of the eight organizations, coordinate through one PostgreSQL
database.\footnote{\url{https://www.postgresql.org}}
Second, we build a workflow baseline that follows the DV~100 doctrine and has no
language model in the loop. Both systems run the same scenarios and write to
the same append-only event log, and one deterministic scorer reads that log for
both. Third, we compare the two on seven scenarios under a pre-registered
protocol, and we report what we learn in whichever direction the result falls.

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:intro:rq}

The thesis asks three research questions.

\begin{description}
\item[RQ1] Can a database-centric agentic architecture coordinate a nursing
  home evacuation better than a scripted workflow baseline?
\item[RQ2] If it cannot, why not? Where does the deficit come from?
\item[RQ3] What would have to change, and what should a designer consider,
  when building an agentic simulation for emergency coordination?
\end{description}

RQ1 has a boundary that we state here and keep throughout the thesis. None of
our scenarios required adaptation. A controller that ignores every disruption
reaches the same score as the baseline (Chapter~\ref{ch:critique}). We
therefore answer RQ1 for these scenarios only. The question is whether the
agentic architecture won here, and we make no claim about whether it can ever
win. RQ2 then looks for the cause of the result in the evidence, and RQ3 turns
that cause into guidance for the next design.

Building the two systems also raises four engineering questions. They support
the research questions.

\begin{enumerate}
\item How can the actors, states, events and dependencies of an evacuation be
  modelled in a database? (Chapter~\ref{ch:data})
\item How can agents that represent different organizations be integrated into
  one system? (Chapter~\ref{ch:agentic})
\item Which persistent structures can serve both runtime control and
  evaluation? (Sections~\ref{sec:data:eventlog}
  and~\ref{sec:data:evalbackbone})
\item How do the agentic system and the workflow baseline compare in
  performance? (Chapters~\ref{ch:eval} and~\ref{ch:results})
\end{enumerate}

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:intro:contributions}

The thesis makes four contributions.

\paragraph{An agentic architecture and its baseline.} We designed and built a
database-centric agentic architecture for evacuation coordination across
several organizations. Ten agents represent people at eight organizations.
PostgreSQL is the single source of truth, and it also acts as the message
broker that wakes the agents. Next to it we built a workflow baseline grounded
in the DV~100 doctrine. The two share the scenarios, the action set and the
scoring, and they differ mainly in how decisions are made. In the baseline, a
simulator also stands in for the field agents
(Chapters~\ref{ch:agentic}, \ref{ch:workflow} and~\ref{ch:data}).

\paragraph{A scored comparison with a negative result.} To our knowledge, this
is the first scored head-to-head comparison of the two approaches on a common
evacuation case. It covers 310 runs over seven scenarios, with pre-registered
hypotheses and deterministic scoring (Chapter~\ref{ch:eval}). The agentic
architecture does not outperform the scripted baseline, and both confirmatory
hypotheses fail. Our main test, Gate~1, compares resident survival with the
baseline in each cell, one model on one scenario, run up to ten times
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:gate1}). It fails in 26 of 27 cells. Three of them are
on the road closure, where the two systems ran different versions of the
scenario. Without them, 23 of 24 cells fail. On the five novel disruptions,
written after the baseline was frozen, the baseline sheltered every resident
in all 50 runs. The best agentic model reached a capped mean survival of 0.894
(Section~\ref{sec:res:novel}). The agentic system also scores below a null
policy, a control that ignores every disruption. In all 15 novel cells it did
worse than this control, and 68 of 150 runs ended worse than doing nothing
(Section~\ref{sec:crit:regret}). Part of this gap is there even without a
disruption.

\paragraph{A diagnosis of the loss.} A held-out decision rubric scores what
each system decided with 16 checks on the event log, fixed before the novel
runs (Section~\ref{sec:eval:heldout}). The agents matched the baseline on 5
checks and fell short on 5. Their messages got through. Over 1,932 addressed requests,
93.8\% of the handshakes were closed. Residents were lost on legs that were
dispatched but not carried through to delivery. The losses come in
discrete amounts. Of 150 novel runs, 102 delivered everyone, 19 delivered
50, one bus-load short, and 18 delivered 90. Runs that rewrote their plan more
often delivered less. Full delivery falls from 90\% at the fewest
plan revisions to 32\% at six or more. Our leading hypothesis is a failure
of commitment. We did not measure comprehension, and we do not claim
a causal direction. Live LLM field agents carried out the orders where
the baseline used a simulator, so a lost leg may have been dropped in the
field.

\paragraph{A method for a fair comparison.} The shared append-only event log is
the backbone of the comparison. Both systems write to it, one scorer reads it,
and every run can be replayed and audited. We also propose Gate~0, a check on
treatment validity. Before a campaign starts, it tests whether a control that
ignores a disruption scores worse than a system that responds to it (our own
novel arm fails it, Section~\ref{sec:crit:gate0}).

\section{Scope and Delimitations}
\label{sec:intro:scope}

Some limits are part of the design. The residents are a passive count and make
no decisions of their own. The flood itself is not simulated. It enters the
case as a forecast and a deadline. The baseline is plain Python and uses no
workflow engine such as Camunda, Temporal or Airflow. Apps for citizens are
outside the scope. The study covers one facility, one city and one hazard.

Other limits come from what was run. Every run uses one simulation time scale,
a \code{SIM_TIME_SCALE} of 8.4, so hypothesis H4 about the effect of time scale
was not tested. Each cell has $k = 10$ replicates, and the registered sign
test runs over only five scenarios, which is too few for it to detect a
moderate effect. Three models completed the full
novel arm: glm-5.2, deepseek-v4-pro and qwen3.5. The third model slot changed
after data were seen, so the model set of the novel arm is exploratory.
Chapter~\ref{ch:critique} discusses each of these limits and the position we
take on it.

\section{Thesis Outline}
\label{sec:intro:outline}

Chapter~\ref{ch:background} reviews scripted coordination, LLM agents, the
limits of LLM planning and coordination through shared state, and places the
thesis among related work. Chapter~\ref{ch:casestudy} presents the Hamburg
case, the organizations involved and the disruption scenarios.
Chapters~\ref{ch:agentic} to~\ref{ch:ui} describe what we built: the agentic
system, the workflow baseline, the data architecture that both share, and the
interface we use to observe a run. Chapter~\ref{ch:eval} sets out the
pre-registered method, and Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports the results.
Chapter~\ref{ch:critique} reads the results back against the protocol and
derives Gate~0 from what it finds.
Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} interprets the results and answers RQ1 and RQ2.
Chapter~\ref{ch:design} answers RQ3 with design considerations for agentic
emergency simulation. Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} summarizes the thesis and
names the next experiment.
