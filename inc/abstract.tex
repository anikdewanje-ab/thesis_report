\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Nursing homes near the coast have to move their residents before a storm surge
arrives. Such an evacuation needs several organizations to work together, and
the plan often changes while it runs. Simulations of this task are usually
scripts that follow written doctrine. They cover only the disruptions their
authors expected. Agents built from large language models (LLMs) could, in
principle, also respond to disruptions that nobody scripted.

In this thesis we design a database-centric agentic architecture and evaluate
it against a scripted baseline. The case is the evacuation of the Augustinum
nursing home in Hamburg, with 100 residents, two buses with 50 seats each and
two school shelters with 60 beds each. Ten LLM agents represent people at eight
organizations. They coordinate through one PostgreSQL database, which holds all
state and wakes each agent when it has work. The baseline follows the German
DV~100 doctrine and uses no language model. Both systems write to one shared
append-only event log, and one deterministic scorer reads it. We compared them
on seven scenarios under a pre-registered protocol.

Both pre-registered hypotheses failed. The baseline sheltered every resident in
all 50 novel runs, and the test on resident survival failed in 26 of 27 cells.
The best agentic configuration reached a mean survival of 0.894. The agentic
system also did worse than a null policy that ignores every disruption. No
scenario required adaptation, however, so the baseline's perfect score says
nothing about its ability to adapt. The agents read the disruptions correctly,
and 93.8\% of 1,932 requests between them ended in a closed handshake. The loss
came from residents who were never dispatched. It arrived in whole bus-loads
and grew with the number of plan revisions. We therefore describe it as a
failure of commitment rather than of comprehension. Decision quality is split.
Over 16 rubric criteria the agentic models match the baseline on five and fall
short on five.

From these results we derive design considerations for agentic emergency
simulation. A leg that is already on the road should stay committed, every
delivery should be confirmed, and scenarios should let a disruption change the
world that is scored. The next experiment is an agentic variant with a
commitment boundary, run on the same scenarios.
