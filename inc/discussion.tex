\section{RQ1: Did the Agentic Architecture Win?}
\label{sec:disc:interpretation}

RQ1 asked whether a database-centric agentic architecture can coordinate an
evacuation better than a scripted workflow baseline. On these scenarios it
could not. None of them required adaptation, so we answer RQ1 as ``did the
agentic architecture win here'' and say nothing about whether it can ever win.
It lost to the baseline on all five novel disruptions. On the
anticipated arm, only glm-5.2 on the undisrupted \code{closed_loop} was shown
to be no worse than the baseline (Section~\ref{sec:res:findings}). We leave
the road closure out of this reading, because there the two systems ran
different versions of the scenario.

The baseline's perfect score does not mean that it adapted. Its disruptions
arrived as messages and changed no world state, so a controller that ignores
them scores the same (Section~\ref{sec:crit:defect}). The agentic side gains
nothing from this reading, because its models lost residents on scenarios
where the correct plan needed no change, and many of their runs ended below doing nothing
(Section~\ref{sec:crit:regret}). Part of that gap has nothing to do with the
disruption. deepseek-v4-pro already loses 21 residents per 100 on the
undisrupted \code{closed_loop}, while glm-5.2 loses none there.

The choice of model changes how large the loss is, but every model still
loses (Table~\ref{tab:res:rd}). The models also lose in different ways.
deepseek-v4-pro and glm-5.2 lose mostly residents who are never delivered.
qwen3.5 leaves far fewer of them, and most of its loss comes from residents
who arrive late or who are not counted because a shelter was filled past its
declared beds (Section~\ref{sec:res:loss:nevermoved}). We tested only
three models with a complete novel arm, so we cannot say whether another
model would close the gap. The design shows no capability threshold, and it
does not support extrapolating beyond qwen3.5.

\section{RQ2: Why the Agentic System Lost}
\label{sec:disc:mechanism}

RQ2 asked why the agentic architecture fell short. Our first analysis, under
Protocol~v1, placed the loss ``at the handshakes'': a driver's completion call
that times out and is never retried, or a facility query that is never
answered. That explanation had not been measured. Protocol~v2 measured it,
and the measurement did not support it. Handshake closure is 0.938
(Section~\ref{sec:res:loss:handshakes}). For two of the three models most of
the loss is residents who were never delivered, although the missions for
them were dispatched. Table~\ref{tab:disc:ruledout} lists the other causes we
tested against the 150 agentic novel runs. These tests were designed after
the data were seen, so they are exploratory.

What remains is that legs already dispatched were not carried through to a
drop-off. Two patterns in the results support this. First, the loss comes in
discrete amounts (Figure~\ref{fig:res:delivery}). A slow loss of quality would
spread the runs out. Dropped legs leave a clustered pattern, with spikes at 50 and 90 delivered residents and gaps around them. The
spike at 50 matches one bus-load, while the spike at 90 matches none. Second,
runs that rewrote their plan more often delivered less. Full delivery falls
from 90\% at the minimum number of plan revisions to 32\% at six or more
(Figure~\ref{fig:res:commitment}).

Our leading hypothesis is a \textbf{commitment failure}. It fits better than a
failure of comprehension, but we cannot rule out a failure in the field (see
below). The agents had the right population, addressed the right
parties and got their messages answered, and on five rubric criteria they
decided as the baseline did. In agent theory, an intention is a plan the agent
keeps until it has a reason to drop it \parencite{rao1995}. In our runs, legs were
re-planned while in flight, and the coordinator often could not establish
whether a leg had finished. One of its abort messages reads ``zero deliveries
across 8 transport plan revisions''. Re-planning on disruption is the
capability the agentic design advertises, and on these scenarios the correct
number of re-plans was zero.

The rubric shows the same pattern from another side
(Figure~\ref{fig:res:rubric}). The five criteria where the agentic models
match the baseline are about deciding: informing the authority, committing the
reserve, planning for the whole population. Three of the five where they fall short
are about closing the loop on a decision. The medical emergency shows
the same gap on a criterion the baseline also fails. Only two of the twenty
qwen3.5 and glm-5.2 runs sent a medical team to the shelter the bus was
bound for.

We did not measure comprehension directly. The rubric does not read message
text, so the evidence comes from behaviour. Two rubric results fit the
commitment reading less well. No agentic model was judged to answer the
facility in time in any run (\code{facility_answered_in_time}, 0
\texttt{yes} out of 10 for each model). On the partial loss of shelter
capacity, half of the qwen3.5 runs gave an order that raised a shelter above
its beds (\code{no_order_raises_shelter_above_beds}, 5 of 10). Each result
could come from a report the agent misread, or from an order it never
updated. The rubric cannot tell the two apart, so these results neither
confirm nor rule out a failure of comprehension.

The causal direction is also open, because a run that is going badly may
re-plan for that reason. Of 108 aborts, 57 (52.8\%) are stand-downs after a
failure, 14 (13.0\%) are clearly started by a re-plan, and 37 (34.3\%) are
other. A commitment-constrained variant of the system would settle the
direction (Section~\ref{sec:concl:future}).

Finally, the design cannot separate the command post from the field agents.
The coordinator's abort messages name failures on both sides, for example
``Hochbahn unresponsive to 3 redispatch requests over 18 min'' and ``bus-1
found no residents staged at the pickup''. We did not trace each lost leg to
the agent that dropped it. In the baseline a simulator carries out the orders,
so the baseline never meets this kind of failure (deviation~7 in
Table~\ref{tab:eval:deviations}). A lost leg may therefore have been dropped
by the command post or by a live field agent.

\paragraph{Relation to prior work.} This pattern is consistent with the
literature reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:bg:llmlimits}.
\textcite{kambhampati2024} argue that an LLM cannot reliably check its own
plans and needs an external verifier. \textcite{huang2024} and
\textcite{stechly2024} find that self-correction without outside feedback
often makes results worse. In our system each re-plan is such a
self-correction, and the coordinator often could not confirm whether a leg had
finished. \textcite{cemri2025} name poor checking of task completion as one of
three main failure groups in multi-agent systems. Our data fit these findings,
but they cannot show that the same mechanisms were at work here.

\section{Cost and Transferability}
\label{sec:disc:tradeoffs}

The baseline wins on response time, cost and determinism, and in our
evaluation it also won the primary outcome. The agentic system needs nine to
ten times as many messages per resident delivered, and most of its runs
overran the time budget (Table~\ref{tab:res:surface}). On these scenarios that
cost bought no survival. Whether it pays off on a scenario that requires
adaptation is a question this thesis cannot answer.

Other emergency domains share the two conditions the case was built around: a
space of possible disruptions too large to write down in advance, and many
organisations that each hold local information. What carries over to them is
our method and our diagnosis. The method is the shared event log, the
treatment-validity gate and the regret against a null policy. The diagnosis is
that the agents' plans were not carried through, and that this fits the
evidence better than a misreading of the situation.

\paragraph{Lessons learned.}\label{sec:disc:lessons} If we did the study again,
we would keep the shared event log. Every instrument of Protocol~v2, including
the handshake closure, the loss attribution and the regret, was written after
the campaign. We could still compute them all, because every message and
decision of both systems was already in that log. Chapter~\ref{ch:design} sets
out what we would change.
