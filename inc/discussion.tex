In this chapter we interpret the results. The numbers are in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} and the corrections to the protocol are in
Chapter~\ref{ch:critique}, so we refer to them here. One boundary applies
throughout. No scenario in this thesis required adaptation. We therefore
answer RQ1 as ``did the agentic architecture win here'', and we say nothing
about whether it can ever win.

\section{What the Result Means}
\label{sec:disc:interpretation}

\paragraph{RQ1.} RQ1 asked whether a database-centric agentic architecture
can coordinate an evacuation better than a scripted workflow baseline. On
these scenarios the answer is no, on both arms of the evaluation. On the
anticipated disruptions the agentic system was not even as good as the
baseline. Non-inferiority holds in only 1 of 8 cells, glm-5.2 on the
undisrupted \code{closed_loop}. On the novel disruptions, where we expected
the agentic system to win, none of the 19 cells passes. The baseline sheltered
every resident in all 50 novel runs, while the best agentic configuration
reached a capped mean survival of 0.894. Across both arms, Gate~1 fails in 26
of 27 cells.

The baseline's perfect score does not mean that it adapted. The disruptions in
our scenarios arrive as messages and do not change the world state, so the
baseline drove the same 60.63 bus-km with and without them. A null policy,
which is the baseline with re-planning switched off, scores the same. The
baseline's regret against that policy is therefore zero in every run, and its
plan was never touched by any disruption.

This reading does not help the agentic side, because its loss cannot be
blamed on a hard scenario. Where the correct plan needed no change, the
agentic models lost between 10 and 27 residents per 100. Their regret is
negative in all 15 novel cells, and 68 of 150 runs ended worse than doing
nothing (Section~\ref{sec:crit:regret}).

\paragraph{RQ2.} RQ2 asked why the agentic architecture fell short. The
agents lost residents because they did not carry their plans through. Their
understanding of the situation was sound. We tested and ruled out the other
candidate causes: wrong population, a failing message layer, too few missions,
a parked reserve bus, the scorer and the drivers' headcounts
(Table~\ref{tab:crit:ruledout}). The loss also comes in whole bus-loads
(Figure~\ref{fig:res:delivery}). This is the pattern a dropped leg leaves,
while a slow loss of quality would spread the results more evenly. In
addition, runs that rewrote their plan more often delivered less. Full
delivery falls from 90\% at the minimum number of plan revisions to 32\% at
six or more (Figure~\ref{fig:res:commitment}). This runs against the
architecture's own promise. Its main feature is re-planning when a disruption
arrives, and in our scenarios the right number of re-plans was zero. We do
not claim the causal direction, because a failing run may also re-plan
because it is failing.

The rubric shows the same pattern from another side
(Figure~\ref{fig:res:rubric}). The agentic models match the baseline on five
criteria, and those are about deciding: informing the authority, committing
the reserve, planning for the whole population. They fall short on five that
are about finishing what was decided. In the medical emergency, the agents
named the right action in their reasoning, and in 16 of 20 runs no medical
order left the agent.

The choice of model changes how large the loss is, but every model still
loses. The risk difference is the agentic survival minus the baseline
survival, so a value of $-0.10$ means ten fewer residents sheltered per 100.
Averaged over the five novel disruptions it is $-0.27$ for deepseek-v4-pro,
$-0.16$ for glm-5.2 and $-0.10$ for qwen3.5. deepseek-v4-pro is the weakest of
the three on every disruption, and qwen3.5 leads glm-5.2 on four of the five
(Table~\ref{tab:res:rd}). Since the baseline scores the same as the null
policy, these values are also the regret of each model. They tell us how many
residents each model's response cost, compared with doing nothing.

The models also fail in different ways. deepseek-v4-pro and glm-5.2 leave
10 to 22.5 residents per 100 who are never moved. qwen3.5 leaves only 2.0 to
5.0. Most of its loss comes from residents who arrive late or who are not
counted because a shelter was filled past its declared beds
(Section~\ref{sec:res:loss:nevermoved}). A stronger model therefore reduced
the commitment failure without removing it. We tested only three models with
a complete novel arm, so we cannot say whether a still stronger model would
close the gap. The design shows no capability threshold, and it does not
support extrapolating beyond qwen3.5.

\paragraph{Relation to prior work.} This pattern is consistent with the
literature reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:bg:llmlimits}.
\textcite{kambhampati2024} argue that an LLM cannot reliably check its own
plans and needs an external verifier. \textcite{huang2024} and
\textcite{stechly2024} find that self-correction without outside feedback
often makes results worse. In our system each re-plan is such a
self-correction, and the coordinator often could not confirm whether a leg had
finished. \textcite{cemri2025} name poor checking of task completion as one of
three main failure groups in multi-agent systems. Our data fit these findings,
but they cannot show that the same mechanisms were at work here.

\section{Cost and Transferability}
\label{sec:disc:tradeoffs}

The baseline wins on response time, cost and determinism, and in our
evaluation it also won the primary outcome. The agentic system needs nine to
ten times as many messages per resident delivered, about 800\,000 tokens per
run, and 1.8 to 2.7 times the wall-clock that the compressed clock allows
(Section~\ref{sec:res:responsetime}). Here that cost bought nothing in return.
Whether it pays off on a scenario that requires adaptation is a question this
thesis cannot answer.

The case was built around two conditions: a space of possible disruptions too
large to write down in advance, and many organisations that each hold local
information. Other emergency domains share these conditions. What carries over
to such domains is our method and our diagnosis. The method is the shared
event log, the treatment-validity gate and the regret against a null policy.
The diagnosis is that the agents understood the situation and then failed to
carry out their own plans.

\section{Validity and Limitations}
\label{sec:disc:limits}

The parity asymmetries that remain between the two systems favour the
baseline (Section~\ref{sec:crit:limitations}). The one that works against
the baseline, deviation~7, could only have made the margin look smaller
(Section~\ref{sec:wf:world}). The asymmetries may explain part of
the margin, but none of them can reverse it. The reserve bus was used in every
agentic run, so it does not explain the residents who were never dispatched.
Beyond the fairness of the comparison, the results come from one facility, one
city and one hazard. The residents are a passive count, and the population is
homogeneous, so we cannot say anything about equity. The design also had
limits of its own: $k = 10$, one time scale and a novel arm whose third model
changed four times. Table~\ref{tab:crit:limits} gives each limitation with the
position we take on it.

\section{Lessons Learned}
\label{sec:disc:lessons}

If we built and evaluated the system again, we would keep the shared event
log. Every instrument of Protocol~v2 was written after the campaign had
finished, including the handshake closure, the loss attribution and the regret
against the null policy. We could still compute all of them, and reach the
diagnosis in this chapter, because every message and decision of both systems
was already in the same append-only log.

We would also change several things: a commitment boundary on legs in
flight, a delivery confirmation for every leg, Gate~0 before the campaign, an
analysis sized before the runs and a model set fixed in advance.
Chapter~\ref{ch:design} sets these out as design considerations, each with the
measurement behind it.
