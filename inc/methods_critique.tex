In this chapter we read the results back against the protocol that produced
them. When we applied the protocol to its own data, it showed that its primary
arm could not have answered the question it was built for. We report that as a
result in its own right. The protocol found a degenerate treatment with its own
negative control, and we use the same control to define the gate that would
have caught it.

We do not repeat the numbers of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} here. We point to
them and add four things: the treatment-validity gate and its evidence, the
regret against a null policy, the alternative explanations we ruled out, and
our position on each declared limitation.

We designed everything in Sections~\ref{sec:crit:gate0}
to~\ref{sec:crit:mechanism} after we had seen the evaluation data. It is
registered as Protocol~v2 in Amendment~16, and under the pre-registration's
own rules all of it is exploratory. It renames what the first protocol
measured. It leaves every number as it was, and the sign of the result stays
the same.

One boundary holds for the whole chapter. No scenario in this thesis ever
required adaptation. We therefore answer RQ1 as ``did the agentic architecture
win here'', and we make no claim about whether it can ever win.

\section{The Defect}
\label{sec:crit:defect}

We built the novel arm to test H2: does the agentic system recover better than
a scripted workflow when a disruption arrives that the workflow has no branch
for? On that arm the workflow baseline scored $S = 1.000$ in all 50 of its
runs. This perfect score does not show a recovery. It shows invariance, and the
scenario design guaranteed it before we chose any model.

The cause lies in how the scenarios are written. Every event in every one of
the fourteen scenario files is an envelope of the form \texttt{\{at, send\}}:
at a given time, send a given message. No scenario event changes the declared
world state, that is the \code{world}, \code{sensing} or \code{hazard} blocks.
The disruption exists as text in an inbox and nowhere else. When SC1 reports a
partial capacity loss, SC1 keeps declaring 60 beds. When bus-1 is suspended,
bus-1 keeps its seats.

The disruption therefore leaves the baseline's physical plan untouched. It
drives 60.63 bus-km on the undisrupted \code{closed_loop} and the same 60.63
bus-km on each of the five novel disruptions
(Section~\ref{sec:res:responsetime}).

The protocol's own negative control confirms this. The no-replan control of
Section~\ref{sec:eval:noreplan} is the doctrine baseline with re-planning
switched off, so it ignores every disruption. When we ran it against the
scored scenarios, it committed byte-identical orders and scored the identical
level on all five novel files and on \code{closed_loop}.

On the novel arm, then, \textbf{ignoring the disruption is an optimal policy}.
If doing nothing already scores at the ceiling, a test cannot produce evidence
for or against an adaptability hypothesis in either direction. The agentic
system could only reach a non-negative risk difference by also doing nothing,
and an agentic architecture is built to respond.

The first protocol knew the mechanism and wrote it down. As
Section~\ref{sec:eval:noreplan} quotes, it stated that on every scored scenario
the control ``cannot differ from the doctrine in outcome, by construction''. We
used that note as the reason to read the negative control on a separate
fixture. Nothing was hidden. Our mistake was that we never required the gate's
guarantee to hold on the files where we read the hypotheses.

\section{Gate 0: Treatment Validity}
\label{sec:crit:gate0}

Protocol~v2 adds a gate that is read before Gates 1 to 3. It is read once per
scenario, and it does not depend on the system under test:

\begin{quote}
A scenario is admitted to a confirmatory adaptability arm only if a system
that ignores its disruption scores strictly worse on it than one that
responds.
\end{quote}

A scenario that fails Gate~0 may still be run and reported, as a test of base
execution. It may not carry an adaptability claim, and no risk difference
computed on it may be described as evidence about re-planning. The gate is
cheap. It costs two runs of the control per scenario and reuses the no-replan
machinery that the first protocol already built.

We call a disruption \emph{binding} when three conditions hold. Each one
catches a different way in which a scenario can miss what it was written to
test.

\begin{description}
\item[D1, state-mutating.] The disruption changes the declared world state as
  well as an inbox. A purely informational disruption can still bind in
  principle, if the system has to act on the information to succeed. We cannot
  assume that it does.
\item[D2, order-binding.] The null policy commits a different order sequence
  from the responding policy. If the orders are byte-identical, no downstream
  instrument can tell the two apart, whatever it measures.
\item[D3, outcome-binding.] The null policy scores a strictly lower outcome.
  When D2 holds without D3, the plans differ but the scoring function cannot
  see the difference.
\end{description}

A fourth condition applies to the scenario set as a whole.
\textbf{D4, recoverable:} at least one policy must reach the feasibility
ceiling. If no policy can recover from a disruption, the score measures the
hazard and says little about the coordinator.

Every scored row reads ``discriminates: no''. On the five novel disruptions
the two variants commit the same orders, drive the same kilometres and reach
the same level. They fail all three conditions.

\code{road_closure} fails in a different way. It is the one scored file where
the null policy commits different orders, 83.1 km against 38.1 km, so it
passes D2. But the scenario declares no \code{world}. The dropoffs are injected
by the scenario instead of being earned by the plan, and completeness comes out
the same whatever was ordered. The disruption changes the orders and leaves the
outcome as it was, so the scenario fails on D3.

\code{closed_loop} carries no disruption, so Gate~0 does not apply to it. We
list it for completeness, and its result is not affected.

\textbf{None of the six disruption scenarios is admitted.} The only scenario in
the repository that passes is the shelter-closure fixture, where the doctrine
reaches level 3 and the null policy level 0. That property is why the first
protocol chose it as the discrimination fixture, and the evaluation set lacks
it.

The gate itself is the contribution we claim here. The first protocol had a
negative control and read it on a fixture. Protocol~v2 requires every
disruption to pass the negative control before it can be scored as a
disruption at all. A check on the instrument thereby becomes a check on every
treatment. How to write a scenario that passes the gate is a design question,
and Section~\ref{sec:design:gate0} takes it up.

\section{What Is Withdrawn and What Stands}
\label{sec:crit:withdrawn}

\subsection{Withdrawn}

\paragraph{H2's verdict, as a statement about adaptability.} The sentence
``the workflow baseline recovers better from unanticipated disruption'' is not
supported, and this design could not have supported it. The disruption never
disturbed the baseline, so there was nothing for it to recover from. Nowhere
in this thesis do we describe the baseline as having adapted, recovered or
re-planned better.

\paragraph{H1's verdict on \texttt{road\_closure}.} The same reason applies at one
remove. The disruption changes the orders but leaves the outcome as it was
(Section~\ref{sec:crit:gate0}), so a survival comparison on it says nothing
about re-planning.

\paragraph{The scope is wider than the novel arm.} Gate~0 fails on all seven
scored scenarios, including the two anticipated ones. We started from a
question about the novel arm, and the evidence supports the wider withdrawal.

\subsection{Not Replaced by an Agentic Win}

The withdrawal works in one direction only. The baseline's score tells us
nothing about adaptation, and that does not make the agentic score look
better. The agentic models lost between 10 and 27 residents per 100 on
scenarios where the correct plan needed no change at all. The scenario design
explains why the baseline's score carries no information. It does not explain
why the agentic score is low.

\subsection{Standing, Unchanged}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Every number.} Nothing in Protocol~v2 regenerates the scores.
  $S$, every risk difference, every recovery level and all 27 Gate~1 verdicts
  are byte-identical before and after.
\item \textbf{The agentic deficit.} It is real and large, and the next
  subsection makes it sharper.
\item \textbf{\code{closed_loop}.} It has no disruption, so the question there
  is base execution. glm-5.2's non-inferiority pass on it stands in full.
\item \textbf{The decision rubric.} It reads the structure of decisions and
  does not use the null policy's score. Its split verdict stands as
  Section~\ref{sec:res:quality} reports it, with parity on five criteria and a
  baseline advantage on five.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Regret Against the Null Policy}
\label{sec:crit:regret}

The first protocol compared the two systems only with each other. It did not
measure how each system scores against doing nothing, and that quantity would
have exposed the problem. Protocol~v2 adds it as null-policy regret,
\begin{equation}
R = S_{\text{system}} - S_{\text{null}},
\label{eq:crit:regret}
\end{equation}
measured per run, on survival, against the no-replan control. $R > 0$ means
responding helped. $R = 0$ means responding bought nothing. $R < 0$ means the
system harmed itself by responding. We use survival instead of level because
the null-policy runs are capped at level 3 while scored runs reach level 4, and
a level difference would then be an artifact of the cap.

shows them per run. The two systems tell different stories.

\textbf{The baseline's regret is zero in all 50 runs.} It gained nothing by
responding, because its response to each disruption changed no order and no
kilometre. The headline ``the workflow baseline beats the agentic system on
resident survival in every disruption tested'' is arithmetically true. But in
that contest the baseline never had to compete.

\textbf{The agentic regret is negative in every cell.} The mean is below zero
in all 15 model and disruption cells. Of 150 runs, three improved on the null
policy, 79 tied it, and \textbf{68 came out worse than doing nothing}. This is
harsher than what the first protocol said. There, the agentic system lost to a
better system. Under regret, it lost to inaction. A negative regret is a
self-inflicted loss, so we cannot blame it on the scenario being hard.

The capability ordering of Section~\ref{sec:res:models} survives intact:
deepseek-v4-pro at $-0.273$, glm-5.2 at $-0.160$, qwen3.5 at $-0.100$. Read as
regret, each value says how much damage the model's response did.

\section{The Mechanism, Corrected by Measurement}
\label{sec:crit:mechanism}

Our first write-up explained the agentic deficit in prose. It said the agents
lost residents ``at the handshakes'': a driver's completion call that times out
and is never retried, a facility query that is never answered. The diagnosis
was plausible, but we had never measured it. Protocol~v2 measured it, and the
measurement did not support it.

\subsection{What the Instruments Showed}

Chapter~\ref{ch:results} already reports these numbers, and we only collect
them here. Handshake closure is 0.938 over 1\,932 addressed requests, and the
three highest-volume handshakes close at 0.947, 0.970 and 0.997
(Section~\ref{sec:res:loss:handshakes}). The dominant loss mechanism is
residents who were never dispatched, with $r = -0.745$ against survival. The
loss comes in whole bus-loads (Figure~\ref{fig:res:delivery}). The
full-delivery rate falls from 90\% at the minimum number of plan revisions to
32\% at six or more (Table~\ref{tab:res:commitment}).

So the messages arrived and were answered. What went wrong was the agents'
decision about which residents to move.

\subsection{The Alternatives We Ruled Out}

Table~\ref{tab:crit:ruledout} lists every other cause we tested against the
150 agentic novel runs, with the verdict on each.

What remains is a \textbf{commitment failure, not a comprehension failure}.
The agents understood the disruption, addressed the right parties and got
their messages answered. They could not carry one committed plan through to
delivery. Legs were re-planned while in flight, and the coordinator often
could not establish whether a leg had finished. The coordinator's own abort
messages say so, for example ``bus-2 reassigned to immediate emergency
dispatch, original mission never delivered'' and ``zero deliveries across 8
transport plan revisions''.

This works against the architecture's own claim. Re-planning on disruption is
the capability the agentic design advertises, and on these scenarios the
correct number of re-plans was zero.

\subsection{What Is Not Established}

We do not claim a causal direction. A run that is going badly may re-plan
because it is going badly. Nothing in an observational arm separates that from
re-planning causing the loss.

We classified each abort by its stated reason. Of 108 aborts, 57 (52.8\%) are
explicit stand-downs after a failure, 14 (13.0\%) are clearly started by a
re-plan, and 37 (34.3\%) are other. The order of classification matters. A
stand-down message often names the re-dispatch attempts that came before it,
for example ``Hochbahn unresponsive to 3 redispatch requests over 18 min''.
Such a message follows from the failure and did not cause it, so we test for a
failure response first. Testing for churn first would misclassify these
messages and inflate a causal reading that the design cannot support.

The experiment that would settle the direction is a
\textbf{commitment-constrained variant}. It caps the number of re-plans, or
forbids cancelling a leg that is already in flight, and runs on the same
scenarios with the same models. It is one campaign and needs no new
scenarios. We see it as the most valuable follow-up to this analysis, and we
leave it to future work.

\subsection{Measuring Handshakes}

Closure was harder to measure than it looks. Three reading rules carry the
instrument, and dropping any one of them silently zeroes a whole handshake
type. First, agent ids are written two ways. A driver addressed as
\code{agent-bus-driver-1} signs as \code{bus-driver-1}, and exact string
matching reports closure as 0.50 instead of 0.94. Second, the message
\code{confirm_dropoff} has two jobs. To a shelter it asks for intake, while to
Hochbahn it only reports progress and expects no answer. Third, no clock in the
log orders two agents' rows, because each recipient stamps its own simulated
time. We therefore define closure as a matching between requests and replies,
which is stable under any ordering. For the same reason we report no reply
latency.

\section{The Pre-Registered Primary Was Underpowered by Construction}
\label{sec:crit:power}

\paragraph{A timing deviation.} The pre-registration named a Clopper-Pearson
interval, a scenario-level sign test and a Holm-Bonferroni correction as the
primary test for H2. None of the three existed in the code when we first
wrote the H2 verdict. We implemented them afterwards, once the result was
known (Amendment~15). Under the pre-registration's own rules that analysis is
exploratory. It agrees with the verdict it was checking
(Section~\ref{sec:res:novel:prereg}), which does not excuse the timing.

\paragraph{A design finding.} At $k = 10$ the registered aggregation could not
reach conventional significance however the data fell. A two-sided exact sign
test on five scenarios cannot return a $p$ below 0.0625, even when every
scenario points the same way. Ties push the floor higher. qwen3.5 and glm-5.2
each tie two scenarios, which leaves three untied observations and a floor of
0.250. Table~\ref{tab:res:signtest} shows that both models sit at the smallest
$p$ their data could have produced. Had a moderate effect existed, the design
would have missed it.

We report this as a finding about pre-registered analysis plans in general. A
registered primary test that cannot reach significance does not test anything.
Protocol~v2 draws three consequences from it.

\begin{enumerate}
\item The registered primary must be one the design can detect. The paired
  risk difference, which is continuous and far more efficient, becomes
  primary. The sign test becomes a supporting robustness check.
\item The minimum detectable effect is stated before any run. If it exceeds
  the registered margin, either $k$ rises or the claim is dropped, and this
  happens before data collection.
\item Cells that fail Gate~0 are excluded from confirmatory arms by a fixed
  rule, decided before the result is known.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The Cut-Point Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{sec:crit:sensitivity}

The protocol made a sensitivity analysis on the level cut points a
precondition on the headline. The summary stated that it must be reported,
yet printed no table, and the reporting script never read the field. We added
the analysis after the first write-up (Amendment~15).

It supports the headline (Section~\ref{sec:res:sensitivity}). With the cut
moved to 0.40, 0.50 and 0.60, every cell in the three full novel arms keeps
its median level except one, deepseek on \code{unverified_persons_report},
which moves from 2.0 to 1.5 at 0.60. No ordering between the agentic system
and the baseline moves anywhere in the range. From this we take a procedural
lesson. A precondition that a report asserts should also be computed by the
report, and the build should fail when it is missing.

We close the chapter with six points.

\begin{enumerate}
\item We ran the comparison on scenarios where the baseline could not lose.
  Its perfect survival reflects invariance, and the no-replan control shows
  this on every scored file.
\item The agentic deficit is still real, and regret measures it more sharply.
  Against the null policy the agentic regret is negative in every cell, and 68
  of 150 runs came out worse than doing nothing.
\item The first diagnosis named the wrong mechanism. The message layer closes
  94\% of addressed requests, and the loss is dominated by residents never
  dispatched.
\item The agents failed at commitment, while their understanding of the
  disruption held. The loss comes in whole bus-loads, and full delivery falls
  from 90\% to 32\% as plan revisions rise. The causal direction is not
  established.
\item The architecture costs an order of magnitude more to coordinate
  (Section~\ref{sec:res:responsetime}).
\item We do not know whether that cost ever buys anything, because no scenario
  in this thesis required it to. A campaign on scenarios that pass Gate~0 is
  the experiment that would find out.
\end{enumerate}
