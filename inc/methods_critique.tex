In this chapter we read the results back against the protocol that produced
them. Applied to its own data, the protocol showed that its primary arm could
not have answered the question it was built for. We report that as a result,
and we use the protocol's own negative control to define a gate that would
have caught it.

Everything in Sections~\ref{sec:crit:gate0} to~\ref{sec:crit:regret} was
designed after we had seen the evaluation data. It is registered as
Protocol~v2 in Amendment~16 and is exploratory, because choices made after
seeing the data can bias a result even without intent \parencite{gelman2014}.
It leaves every number of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} as it was. No scenario in
this thesis ever required adaptation, so we answer RQ1 as ``did the agentic
architecture win here'' and make no claim about whether it can ever win.

\section{The Defect}
\label{sec:crit:defect}

We built the novel arm to test H2: does the agentic system recover better than
a scripted workflow when a disruption arrives that the workflow has no branch
for? On that arm the workflow baseline scored $S = 1.000$ in all 50 of its
runs. This score shows invariance and no recovery, and the scenario design
guaranteed it before we chose any model.

The cause lies in how the scenarios are written. Every event in every one of
the fourteen scenario files is an envelope of the form \texttt{\{at, send\}}:
at a given time, send a given message. There are fourteen files, one per
scored scenario for each of the two systems. No event changes the declared world
state, that is the \code{world}, \code{sensing} or \code{hazard} blocks. When
SC1 reports a partial capacity loss, SC1 keeps declaring 60 beds. When bus-1 is
suspended, bus-1 keeps its seats. The baseline's physical plan is therefore
untouched. It drives 60.63 bus-km on the undisrupted \code{closed_loop} and the
same 60.63 bus-km on each of the five novel disruptions
(Section~\ref{sec:res:responsetime}).

The protocol's own negative control \parencite{lipsitch2010} confirms this.
The no-replan control of Section~\ref{sec:eval:noreplan} is the doctrine
baseline with re-planning switched off, so it ignores every disruption. On all
five novel files and on \code{closed_loop} it committed byte-identical orders
and scored the identical level. On the novel arm, ignoring the disruption is
an optimal policy. A test where doing nothing already scores at the ceiling
cannot produce evidence for or against an adaptability hypothesis. The first
protocol knew this and wrote it down (Section~\ref{sec:eval:noreplan}). Our
mistake was that we never required the control to discriminate on the files
where we read the hypotheses.

\section{Gate 0: Treatment Validity}
\label{sec:crit:gate0}

Protocol~v2 adds a gate that is read once per scenario, before Gates 1 to 3,
and independent of the system under test:

\begin{quote}
A scenario is admitted to a confirmatory adaptability arm only if a system
that ignores its disruption scores strictly worse on it than one that
responds.
\end{quote}

A scenario that fails Gate~0 may still be run as a test of base execution, but
it may not carry an adaptability claim. The gate costs two runs of the control
per scenario. We call a disruption \emph{binding} when three conditions hold.

\begin{description}
\item[D1, state-mutating.] The disruption changes the declared world state,
  and not only an inbox.
\item[D2, order-binding.] The null policy commits a different order sequence
  from the responding policy.
\item[D3, outcome-binding.] The null policy scores a strictly lower outcome.
\end{description}

A fourth condition applies to the scenario set as a whole. \textbf{D4,
recoverable:} at least one policy must reach the feasibility ceiling, or the
score measures the hazard and not the coordinator.

Table~\ref{tab:crit:gate0} holds the evidence. We ran the doctrine baseline and
the no-replan control side by side on the fixture and on all seven scored
scenarios. Both are capped at level 3 here, because neither has a clean-run
reference in this comparison.

\section{What Is Withdrawn and What Stands}

We withdraw H2's verdict as a statement about adaptability. The sentence ``the
workflow baseline recovers better from unanticipated disruption'' is not
supported, because the disruption never disturbed the baseline. H2 still fails
as registered (Section~\ref{sec:res:findings}), but that failure is not
evidence about adaptability in either direction. For the same
reason we withdraw H1's verdict on \code{road_closure} as evidence about
adaptation, since there the disruption changes the orders but not the outcome. No disruption scenario passes Gate~0,
six of six, so the withdrawal reaches beyond the novel arm. Gate~0 does not
apply to \code{closed_loop}, which has no disruption.

The withdrawal works in one direction only. The agentic models lost between 10
and 27 residents per 100 on scenarios where the correct plan needed no change
at all. The scenario design explains why the baseline's score carries no
information about adaptation. It does not explain why the agentic score is
low.

Everything else stands. Protocol~v2 regenerates no score, so $S$, every risk
difference, every recovery level and all 27 Gate~1 verdicts, including those
on \code{road_closure}, are unchanged as numbers. glm-5.2's non-inferiority pass on \code{closed_loop} stands, since that
scenario tests base execution. The decision rubric does not use the null
policy, so its split verdict stands as Section~\ref{sec:res:quality} reports
it.

\subsection{Regret Against the Null Policy}
\label{sec:crit:regret}

The first protocol compared the two systems only with each other. Protocol~v2
also compares each system with doing nothing, as null-policy regret
$R = S_{\text{system}} - S_{\text{null}}$, per run, on survival, against the
no-replan control. $R > 0$ means the system did better than doing nothing,
$R = 0$ means it did as well, and $R < 0$ means it did worse. A negative $R$
does not by itself show that the response caused the loss, because the null
policy also runs on the baseline's execution model. We use
survival because the null-policy runs are capped at level 3, while scored runs
reach level 4. Here $S$ is capped at 1.0, so that arrivals counted above the
ceiling (Section~\ref{sec:res:integrity:ceiling}) cannot show up as help. The
risk differences of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} use $S$ without the cap, which is
why qwen3.5's mean regret here ($-0.106$) differs slightly from its risk
difference ($-0.10$).

novel disruptions, 50 runs per row, with $S$ capped at 1.0. Exploratory.}
\label{tab:crit:regret}

Table~\ref{tab:crit:regret} gives the counts. The baseline's regret is zero in
all 50 runs, because its response changed no order and no kilometre. The
headline that the baseline beats the agentic system on survival in every
novel disruption is arithmetically true, but the baseline never had to compete.

The agentic regret is negative in all 15 model and disruption cells. Of 150
runs, none improved on the null policy, 82 tied it, and \textbf{68 came out
worse than doing nothing}. Without the cap, three qwen3.5 runs would count as
improvements, but only through arrivals counted above the ceiling. Under the
first protocol, the agentic system lost to a better system. Under regret, it
also scored below inaction. Since doing nothing reaches the ceiling, the loss
cannot be blamed on a hard scenario. The model ordering of
Section~\ref{sec:res:models} is unchanged.

Not all of this regret comes from the response to the disruption. The agentic
models also lose residents when there is no disruption at all. On
\code{closed_loop}, glm-5.2 scores 1.000, qwen3.5 0.950 and deepseek-v4-pro
0.790 (Table~\ref{tab:res:anticipated}). Compared with their capped novel
means of 0.840, 0.894 and 0.727, this splits each deficit in two parts. For
deepseek-v4-pro, about 0.21 of its 0.27 is already lost without a disruption,
and only about 0.06 is added on the novel arm. For qwen3.5 the two parts are
about 0.05 and 0.06. glm-5.2 is the clean case. It loses nothing undisrupted
and about 0.16 on the novel arm. This split is descriptive only, because the
\code{closed_loop} cells and the novel cells are different runs. It shows
that regret measures how far a system falls below the null policy. For
deepseek-v4-pro most of that distance is base execution, for qwen3.5 about
half, and for glm-5.2 none.

\section{The Pre-Registered Primary Was Underpowered by Construction}
\label{sec:crit:power}

The registered primary test for H2 was implemented only after the result was
known (Amendment~15), so it is exploratory
(Section~\ref{sec:res:novel:prereg}). The cut-point sensitivity analysis
\parencite{simonsohn2020} was added under the same amendment. The more
important finding is about the design. The sign test takes one observation
per scenario. With five scenarios, a two-sided exact sign test cannot return a
$p$ below $2 \times 0.5^5 = 0.0625$, even when every scenario points the same
way, and this holds for any $k$. Ties push the floor higher. At $k = 10$, a
scenario where five of ten runs go each way counts as a tie. qwen3.5 and
glm-5.2 each tie two scenarios, which leaves a floor of 0.250, and both reached
$p = 0.250$. A registered primary that cannot reach significance does not test
anything, and a moderate effect would have been missed. Protocol~v2 draws
three consequences.

\begin{enumerate}
\item The paired risk difference becomes primary, since reducing an outcome to
  a direction throws power away \parencite{ganesh2018}. The sign test becomes a
  robustness check.
\item The minimum detectable effect \parencite{cohen1988} is stated before any
  run. If it exceeds the registered margin, either $k$ rises or the claim is
  dropped. The sign test, as a robustness check, needs more scenarios, since
  no value of $k$ lowers its floor.
\item Cells that fail Gate~0 are excluded from confirmatory arms by a rule
  fixed before the result is known.
\end{enumerate}

Chapter~\ref{ch:results} discloses further caveats where their effect shows
(Sections~\ref{sec:res:models:levelcap}, \ref{sec:res:integrity:ceiling},
\ref{sec:res:h4} and~\ref{sec:res:summary}). The following apply to every
table.

\begin{itemize}
\item The design itself is small. Each cell has $k = 10$ replicates, only one
  time scale ran, and the third model slot of the novel arm was held by four
  models in turn.
\item The cloud model tags are not version-frozen, so no digest pins these
  results.
\item One run, deepseek-v4-pro on \code{unverified_persons_report} at
  replicate 1000, is flagged for possible straight-line routing during a
  routing-service quota outage.
\item The results come from one facility, one city and one hazard. The
  residents are a passive count of 100, and the population is homogeneous.
\item Equity is therefore undefined. Relief logistics treats equity as an
  objective of its own \parencite{huang2012}, but with a homogeneous
  population a maximum-minimum subgroup difference is always zero.
\item Four asymmetries favour the baseline. Two are parity deviations
  accepted for doctrinal reasons, deviations~5 and~6 in
  Table~\ref{tab:eval:deviations}: the consolidated situation picture, and the
  controller's access to road distance and duration. The other two are the
  anchor of a replacement bus leg and a fallback playbook that mis-frames two
  message types for the agents. We did not measure the size of any of them,
  so we cannot say how much of the gap they explain. The later
  release of the reserve bus may also cost the agentic side time. We treat it
  as a difference in decisions (Section~\ref{sec:wf:fleet}).
\end{itemize}
