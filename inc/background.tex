\chapter{Background and Related Work}
\label{ch:background}

This thesis compares two ways of coordinating an evacuation. One is a script
written in advance. The other is a group of language-model agents that reason
over a shared database. Four lines of research meet here. The first is work on
scripted processes and how they handle events nobody planned for. The second is
work on agents built from large language models (LLMs). The third is a critical
line of work that tests how well such models plan. The fourth is older work on
programs that coordinate through a shared store. We close with the systems that
come closest to ours and the gap this thesis fills.

\section{Scripted Coordination and Unanticipated Events}
\label{sec:bg:workflow}

Emergency services coordinate large incidents through fixed command
structures. The best-studied example is the American Incident Command System
(ICS). Research on ICS describes a tension between two kinds of organization.
A mechanistic organization works by rules and a clear hierarchy. An organic one
adapts its structure to the situation \parencite{chang2017}. ICS sits on the
mechanistic side. Still, \textcite{bigley2001a} show that such a
bureaucratic system can respond in a flexible and reliable way when the
situation keeps changing. The system also has known weak points.
\textcite{buck2006} find that ICS works less well when many independent
organizations must cooperate, and that it has trouble absorbing help that was
not part of the plan. German fire services and civil protection follow a
comparable doctrine, DV~100 \parencite{fwdv100}. Chapter~\ref{ch:workflow}
describes how our baseline implements it.

Research on business processes asks the same question from a software angle.
\textcite{reichert2012} separate two kinds of exceptions. An anticipated
exception is one the designer foresaw, so the process can carry a handler for
it from the start. An unanticipated exception is one nobody foresaw. It needs a
change to the running process, and a fixed script cannot make that change by
itself. Build-time handlers are the state of the art for the first kind.
Worklets, for example, select a prepared sub-process that fits the current
context \parencite{adams2007}.

Resilience engineering draws a similar line. \textcite{woods2015a} separates
robustness from graceful extensibility. Robustness means handling
disruptions that were modelled in advance. Graceful extensibility means
stretching to meet a surprise that falls outside the model. These terms give
the comparison in this thesis its vocabulary. A scripted baseline can be robust
against anticipated disruptions. Whether an agentic system extends further when
the disruption is new is an empirical question, and Chapter~\ref{ch:eval}
describes how we test it.

\section{LLM-Driven Agents}
\label{sec:bg:agentic}

An LLM agent is a language model placed in a loop. It observes its
environment, reasons about what to do, acts, and observes the result. ReAct
interleaves written reasoning steps with actions. The authors argue that the
reasoning helps the model track and update its plan and deal with exceptions
\parencite{yao2023}. Reflexion adds a step in which the agent writes a short
reflection on its own performance and keeps it in memory for the next attempt
\parencite{shinn2023}. The authors also note that this works best when the
agent receives a clear signal about what went wrong. Our agents run a similar
loop of sensing, reasoning, acting and reflecting.

A second line of work gives agents human roles. Generative Agents places 25
agents with memory, reflection and planning in a small simulated town, and
human raters find their social behaviour believable \parencite{park2023}. That
study measures believability. It does not score how well the agents perform a
task under pressure. CAMEL assigns roles through prompts and studies how two
role-playing agents cooperate on a task \parencite{li2023}. Our ten agents
follow the same idea. Each one plays a person in a role at one of eight
organizations.

Several frameworks let many such agents work together. AutoGen builds
applications from agents that talk to each other, to tools and to humans
\parencite{wu2023}. ChatDev splits software development into phases and gives
each phase to agents in fixed roles \parencite{qian2024}. MetaGPT writes human
standard operating procedures into the prompts of role-specialized agents and
passes work along an assembly line \parencite{hong2024}. MetaGPT is
interesting for this thesis because it mixes the two ideas we keep apart. Its
agents reason, but their order of work comes from a script. Recent surveys
group these systems by how they coordinate: through a central controller, in a
hierarchy, or as peers \parencite{tran2025,zhuLLMBasedMultiAgentOrchestration2026a}.

We use none of these frameworks. Our system runs its models through Ollama
inside an engine we wrote in plain asynchronous Python.
Chapter~\ref{ch:agentic} explains why.

\section{Limits of LLM Planning}
\label{sec:bg:llmlimits}

A strong critical literature questions whether LLMs can plan at all.
\textcite{kambhampati2024} argue that an LLM on its own can neither plan
reliably nor check its own plans. In their view, it should propose plans and
leave the checking to a sound external verifier. Benchmark results support
this. Planning performance is weak and uneven across models, and newer
reasoning models improve it without solving it \parencite{valmeekam2024}.

Self-correction is a related concern, because it is how a replanning agent is
supposed to recover. \textcite{huang2024} find that when a model corrects its
own reasoning without outside feedback, the result often gets worse.
\textcite{stechly2024} report the same pattern for planning tasks. Performance
drops when the model criticizes its own answers and rises when a correct
external checker gives the feedback.

Multi-agent systems add failure modes of their own. \textcite{cemri2025} study
execution traces from several popular frameworks and build a taxonomy of how
they fail. The failures fall into three groups: poor system design, poor
alignment between agents, and poor checking of whether the task is done.
Other work doubts that several agents help much. A strong single agent
with good prompts can match a multi-agent discussion \parencite{wang2024}.
When both get the same budget of reasoning tokens, a single agent can match or
beat a multi-agent system \parencite{tran2026}.

For this thesis, the literature predicts that agents may understand a
situation and still fail to act on it well. It also suggests that an LLM
should not judge its own success. Our evaluation therefore
scores every run with deterministic code over the event log, and not with a
model. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} comes back to these failure types when it asks
where the agentic deficit comes from.

\section{Shared-State Coordination}
\label{sec:bg:dbcentric}

Our agents do not call each other directly. They read and write one shared
database, and every change is recorded in an append-only log. The idea of
coordinating through shared state is old. In a blackboard system, independent
knowledge sources watch a common data structure and add to it when they can
contribute \parencite{nii1986}. In the Linda model, processes communicate by
placing tuples in a shared space and taking them out again, without knowing
each other \parencite{gelernter1985}. Event sourcing, a pattern from software
engineering, keeps the full history of changes as the source of truth. Any
past state can then be rebuilt by replaying the log. An industry study finds
this useful for audits and replay but notes that it makes schema changes harder
\parencite{overeem2021}.

\section{Related Work and the Gap}
\label{sec:bg:related}

A small number of recent systems apply LLM agents to disasters. RESPOND couples
a flood forecast with an agent-based model of the population. LLM-driven
agents decide how residents evacuate, look for resources and communicate
\parencite{sultimov2026}. It is a short demonstration paper and does not report
a controlled comparison. \textcite{lee2025} give LLM agents synthetic personas
built from mobility and demographic data, and ask how well they predict real
evacuation decisions. Both works model the people who evacuate. In our system
the residents make no decisions. The agents are the organizations that move
them.

DORA is a benchmark for disaster response. It gives LLM agents operational
tasks that need geospatial tools and scores them against expert-verified
solutions that can be replayed \parencite{wang2026}. Its scoring is close to
ours in spirit. It evaluates models on single tasks, however, and does not
compare an agentic system with a scripted one. \textcite{li2026} ran a long
design study with emergency-preparedness practitioners. They conclude that LLM
agent simulations are more useful for shaping procedures and training than for
predicting outcomes. This matches the purpose of our simulation, which studies
a coordination mechanism and does not forecast a real evacuation.

Evacuation research without LLMs is large. It includes optimization of
citywide transfers of elderly people and agent-based models of people with
reduced mobility. Chapter~\ref{ch:casestudy} cites the studies that bear on our
case.

To our knowledge, no prior work puts LLM agents in the role of the
organizations that coordinate an evacuation and compares them with a
doctrine-grounded scripted baseline on a scored outcome. This thesis makes that
comparison. It holds the world model, the action set and the scoring fixed, and
changes only the way decisions are made (Chapter~\ref{ch:validity}).
