\subsection{Minimum Effect and the Registered Primary}
\label{sec:res:novel:delta}
\label{sec:res:novel:prereg}

H2 also set a minimum effect. It required a median shift of one whole recovery
level, $\delta_m = +1.0$, together with first-order stochastic dominance of the
agentic system over the baseline \parencite{yalonetzky2013}. No cell has a
positive median shift. Every novel row reads \code{below_delta_m} with
dominance assigned to the baseline, so neither condition holds anywhere. At
best the agentic side matches the baseline's median level on four of the five
disruptions, and it still loses residents there. This is why Gate~1 fails
where the median level does not.

The pre-registration named a different primary test for H2. It registered an
exact Clopper-Pearson interval \parencite{clopper1934} on
$P(\text{level}_{\text{agentic}} \geq \text{level}_{\text{baseline}})$ over the
$k$ agentic runs, aggregated across scenarios by a sign test with
Holm-Bonferroni correction \parencite{holm1979}. We implemented that test after
the result was known (Amendment~15), so under the pre-registration's own rules
it is exploratory.

The registered test agrees with the risk-difference verdict. No
Clopper-Pearson interval has a lower bound above 0.5, so no cell puts the
agentic system above chance. One cell falls below chance before correction,
deepseek on \code{conflicting_evacuation_orders} at 1 of 10 with $p = 0.021$.
It does not survive the Holm correction, where $p = 0.107$. At $k = 10$ every
interval is about 0.6 wide, and qwen3.5 and glm-5.2 already sit at the
smallest sign-test $p$ their data could produce, 0.250.
Section~\ref{sec:crit:power} explains why, and why the paired risk difference
carries the argument instead.

\subsection{Cut-Point Sensitivity}
\label{sec:res:sensitivity}

The protocol makes a cut-point sensitivity analysis \parencite{simonsohn2020} a
precondition on the headline. We re-read each cell's median level with the
level 1 to 2 cut moved to 0.40, 0.50 and 0.60. Every cell in the three full
novel arms reads the same median at all three cuts, except deepseek on
\code{unverified_persons_report}, which moves from 2.0 to 1.5 at the 0.60 cut.
The other unstable cells are minimax-m3 on the anticipated arm and two partial
series, kimi-k2.7-code at $n = 3$ and kimi-k3 at $n = 1$. No
agentic-versus-baseline ordering moves anywhere in the cut range, so the
analysis supports the headline.

\subsection{What the Novel Arm Does Not Show}
\label{sec:res:novel:boundary}

The novel arm answers whether the agentic system won on these scenarios. It
does not answer whether an agentic system can ever win, because no scenario in
this thesis ever required adaptation. A controller that ignores every
disruption scores the same level as the baseline on all five novel scenarios
(Section~\ref{sec:crit:defect}). The baseline's $S$ of 1.000 is therefore the
null policy's score, and it is no evidence that the baseline adapted. This
does not turn into an agentic win either. Against the same null policy, 68 of
the 150 agentic runs came out worse than doing nothing
(Section~\ref{sec:crit:regret}).

\subsection{Handshake Closure}
\label{sec:res:loss:handshakes}

Over 1\,932 addressed requests in the agentic novel arm, handshake closure is
\textbf{0.938}. The three highest-volume handshakes close at 0.947, 0.970 and
0.997. The one weak handshake is \code{abort_request} at 0.548, on a small
denominator of 31 requests.

The dominant loss mechanism is residents who were never dispatched at all.
Across the 150 agentic novel runs from the three full models, never-dispatched
residents correlate with $S$ at $r = -0.745$, against $-0.579$ for late
arrivals and $+0.061$ for shelter overload. These are exploratory Protocol~v2
instruments, written after the data were seen. Section~\ref{sec:crit:mechanism}
lists the other explanations we ruled out.

\section{Decision Quality}
\label{sec:res:quality}

A held-out rubric measured decision quality on the novel arm. It is a
secondary, structural measure. It is never tabulated beside a recovery level,
and it does not enter any gate. Each cell counts \texttt{yes} judgements out of
10. Figure~\ref{fig:res:rubric} shows all sixteen criteria across the five
novel disruptions, and Appendix~\ref{app:rubric} gives the counts.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{pics/results-rubric-split.pdf}
\caption[The sixteen rubric criteria]{The sixteen rubric criteria, grouped by
how the two sides compare. The tick marks the baseline. Parity holds where the
agentic markers sit on it, and the baseline's lead is the distance to the left
of it. The bottom group of four is where the baseline itself scores zero. The
last two are the criteria the source leaves unplaced, drawn apart rather than
pushed into a group. Table~\ref{tab:res:rubric} in
Appendix~\ref{app:rubric} gives the counts.}
\label{fig:res:rubric}
\end{figure}

Read across all sixteen criteria, the rubric splits three ways.

\begin{sloppypar}
\textbf{Parity on five criteria.} qwen3.5 and glm-5.2 match the baseline's 10
out of 10 on \code{authority_informed} for both disruptions,
\code{reserve_committed}, \code{whole_population_planned} and
\code{stood_down_after_retraction}. On these criteria the agents reason about
the disruption correctly.

\textbf{The baseline leads on five criteria.} The clearest is
\code{facility_answered_in_time}, where the baseline scores 10 and no agentic
model ever answered the facility in time. On the other four the baseline's 10
compares with qwen3.5, glm-5.2 and deepseek as follows.
\code{no_order_raises_shelter_above_beds} scores 5, 9 and 8,
\code{facility_runs_untouched} 7, 8 and 5,
\code{in_flight_leg_lands_where_ordered} 8, 6 and 6, and
\code{full_evacuation_kept} 9, 10 and 5.
\end{sloppypar}

\textbf{Three criteria indict the scenario.} \code{bus_load_within_cap},
\code{medic_sent_to_the_bus_destination} and
\code{everyone_bedded_at_the_reduced_capacity} score 0 for the baseline too,
so they cannot count as agentic failures. On
\code{shelter_set_down_within_beds} the agentic side beats the baseline, at 2
and 2 against 0.

The zero on \code{medic_sent_to_the_bus_destination} covers two different
behaviours. The baseline never requested medical support. Its holding order
(Section~\ref{sec:wf:fallback}) does not reach a bus already on the road. The
agentic models did read the report. In all twenty runs of qwen3.5 and glm-5.2,
between 16 and 43 reasoning entries per run name the emergency, the resident's
respiratory distress or the medical service. One glm-5.2 run concludes
\enquote{I must immediately alert DRK to intercept bus-1 or proceed to the SC1
dropoff point}, and then sends only a status update. Two of the twenty runs
sent a medical order to the shelter the bus was bound for. One sent it to the
Augustinum, which the bus had already left, sixteen sent none, and one could
not be judged. The criterion therefore measures execution rather than
comprehension.

\textbf{Decision quality is split.} Over the sixteen criteria the agentic
models match the baseline on five and fall short on five. They match it on the
criteria about deciding, and fall short on the criteria about closing the
loop.

\label{sec:res:responsetime}

\section{Model Sweep and Capability Threshold}
\label{sec:res:models}

The gap narrows with model capability, and agent benchmarks also find large
differences between models \parencite{xu2025}. The mean novel-arm risk
difference is $-0.27$ for deepseek-v4-pro, $-0.16$ for glm-5.2 and $-0.10$ for
qwen3.5. deepseek-v4-pro has the largest deficit on all five novel
disruptions, and qwen3.5 the smallest on four of them. The exception is
\code{sc1_partial_capacity_loss}, where glm-5.2 at $-0.19$ does slightly
better than qwen3.5 at $-0.22$. The weaker models fail badly. minimax-m3 scored
0.340 on \code{closed_loop} and 0.150 on \code{road_closure}, and
nemotron-3-ultra scored 0.000 on the two runs it completed. Neither model is
part of the headline result.

\textbf{Hypothesis H3 cannot be resolved, because it presupposes H2.} H3
predicted that the agentic advantage on novel disruptions would be
model-robust, with a capability threshold below which that advantage
disappears. With no advantage to be robust or fragile, the threshold is
undefined. The ordering above is descriptive, and the design does not support
extrapolating it past qwen3.5.

\subsection{Comparability of the Model Series}
\label{sec:res:models:levelcap}
\label{sec:res:models:churn}

A promotion to level 4 needs a resource-efficiency judgement against the
model's own no-disruption reference, which takes three qualifying control runs.
A model without \code{agentic_closed_loop} runs is therefore capped at level 3.
qwen3.5 was at first novel-arm-only and capped by this rule, so Amendment~13
ran its ten \code{closed_loop} cells. After re-scoring it has 30 level-4 runs
and a median level of 4, against glm-5.2's 31 and 4, so levels are comparable
across the three full arms. Re-scoring changed no value of $S$ or $RD$ and no
Gate~1 verdict, because these are computed per run without the reference.
kimi-k3 (11 runs, $S = 0.773$), kimi-k2.7-code (3 runs, 0.667) and gpt-oss
(4 runs, 0.375) remain capped. They are compared on $S$ only and never ranked
by level against the full arms.

The novel arm's third model slot was held in turn by kimi-k3, gpt-oss,
kimi-k2.7-code and finally qwen3.5. Each swap was made after seeing data, so
under the pre-registration the novel arm's model set, and any analysis that
uses it, is exploratory. glm-5.2 and deepseek-v4-pro were fixed before the
novel arm ran and are unaffected.

\section{Integrity of the Reported Numbers}
\label{sec:res:integrity}

We report two measurement defects here instead of correcting them in the
scorer, because a correction after the fact would rewrite a confirmatory
result.

\subsection{Six Runs Shelter More Residents Than the Ceiling Admits}
\label{sec:res:integrity:ceiling}

Three qwen3.5 runs score an impossible $S = 1.1$. Three further runs, one from
qwen3.5 and two from deepseek, have the same defect but stay masked, because
their valid-resident count did not exceed the ceiling. The population caps the
feasibility ceiling at 100, while valid residents are capped only by
per-shelter beds, which can add up to 110. These runs show real over-delivery
credited against a ceiling that does not admit it. We cap $S$ at 1.0 at the
reporting layer, and no table in this thesis shows a value above 1.0.

\subsection{One Time Scale, So H4 Was Not Tested}
\label{sec:res:h4}

Every result in this chapter uses a single \code{SIM_TIME_SCALE} of 8.4. Gate~3
therefore has one point per cell, and no crossover can be located. H4 asked at
which time scale the agentic latency cost overtakes its coordination benefit,
so H4 was not tested, and we make no crossover, trend or scale-dependence
claim. Section~\ref{sec:crit:limitations} collects the caveats that apply to
every table.

\section{Summary of Findings}
\label{sec:res:findings}

\begin{enumerate}
\item The workflow baseline is superior on survival across all seven scenarios.
  Gate~1 fails in 26 of 27 cells. \textbf{H1 is rejected}: non-inferiority
  holds in 1 of 8 anticipated cells, glm-5.2 on the undisrupted
  \code{closed_loop}, and fails in the other 7.
\item \textbf{H2 is rejected outright}, on both of its co-primaries. Zero of 19
  novel Gate~1 cells pass on survival, and no cell reaches the registered
  minimum effect of one recovery level. The registered Clopper-Pearson primary,
  implemented late and therefore exploratory, agrees.
\item \textbf{H3 cannot be resolved}, because it presupposes the advantage H2
  failed to establish. Descriptively, the deficit narrows with model
  capability, from $-0.27$ for deepseek through $-0.16$ for glm-5.2 to $-0.10$
  for qwen3.5.
\item Decision quality is split. Over the sixteen rubric criteria the agentic
  models match the baseline on five and fall short on five.
\item \textbf{H4 was not tested.} Only one time scale was run.
\end{enumerate}

The baseline's win is not evidence that it adapted, and the agentic loss is not
a failure of comprehension. Chapter~\ref{ch:critique} establishes the first,
and Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} argues the second.
