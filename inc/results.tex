This chapter reports what the evaluation measured, under the design frozen in
Chapter~\ref{ch:eval}.

The workflow baseline beats the database-centric agentic system on resident
survival on all five novel disruptions. On the undisrupted \code{closed_loop}
one model ties it. On the road closure the two systems ran different versions
of the scenario, so that comparison is not like-for-like
(Section~\ref{sec:res:anticipated}). Both confirmatory hypotheses, H1 and H2,
fail. Gate~1 fails in 26 of its 27 cells. Three of the failing cells are on
the road closure, and without them Gate~1 fails in 23 of 24.

\subsection{Minimum Effect and the Registered Primary}
\label{sec:res:novel:delta}
\label{sec:res:novel:prereg}

Gate~1 is the first of H2's two co-primaries (Section~\ref{sec:eval:analysis}).
The second is a minimum effect. It required a median shift of one whole recovery
level, $\delta_m = +1.0$, together with first-order stochastic dominance of the
agentic system over the baseline \parencite{yalonetzky2013}. No cell has a
positive median shift. Every novel row reads \code{below_delta_m} with
dominance assigned to the baseline, so neither condition holds anywhere. At
best the agentic side matches the baseline's median level on four of the five
disruptions, and it still loses residents there. A few lost residents lower
$S$ but not the median level, so Gate~1 can fail in a cell where the median
level ties. Both co-primaries fail in every novel cell, so H2 fails.

The pre-registration's primary test for H2, the Clopper-Pearson interval on
$P(\text{level}_{\text{agentic}} \geq \text{level}_{\text{baseline}})$, was
implemented after the result was known (Amendment~15). It is exploratory, and
the verdict above does not rest on it. It agrees with that verdict. No
Clopper-Pearson interval has a lower bound above 0.5, so no cell puts the
agentic system above chance. One cell falls below chance before correction,
deepseek on \code{conflicting_evacuation_orders} at 1 of 10 with $p = 0.021$.
It does not survive the Holm correction, where $p = 0.107$. At $k = 10$ every
interval is about 0.6 wide (Section~\ref{sec:crit:power}).

\subsection{Cut-Point Sensitivity}
\label{sec:res:sensitivity}

The protocol makes a cut-point sensitivity analysis \parencite{simonsohn2020} a
precondition on the headline. We re-read each cell's median level with the
level 1 to 2 cut moved to 0.40, 0.50 and 0.60. Every cell in the three full
novel arms reads the same median at all three cuts, except deepseek on
\code{unverified_persons_report}, which moves from 2.0 to 1.5 at the 0.60 cut.
The other unstable cells are minimax-m3 on the anticipated arm and two partial
series, kimi-k2.7-code at $n = 3$ and kimi-k3 at $n = 1$. No
agentic-versus-baseline ordering moves anywhere in the cut range, so the
analysis supports the headline. Table~\ref{tab:app:levels} gives the median of
every cell at each cut.

runs, and 215 were invalidated because a shelter was filled past its declared
beds.

\subsection{Handshake Closure}
\label{sec:res:loss:handshakes}

Handshake closure is the share of addressed requests that drew a matching
reply (Section~\ref{sec:eval:v2}). Over 1\,932 addressed requests in the
agentic novel arm, it is \textbf{0.938}. The three highest-volume handshakes close at 0.947, 0.970 and
0.997. The one weak handshake is \code{abort_request} at 0.548, on a small
denominator of 31 requests.

For deepseek-v4-pro and glm-5.2, the dominant loss is residents who were never
delivered. Per 100 residents, they account for 18.3 of deepseek-v4-pro's
deficit of 27.3 and 12.8 of glm-5.2's 16.0. For qwen3.5 they account for only
3.1 of 10.0, and lateness (6.9) and overload (4.0) weigh more. qwen3.5's three
parts add up to more than its deficit, because in its over-delivery runs
(Section~\ref{sec:res:integrity}) late and refused arrivals are counted on top
of a full ceiling. Across the 150
agentic novel runs from the three full models, never-delivered residents
correlate with $S$ at $r = -0.745$, against $-0.579$ for late arrivals and
$+0.061$ for shelter overload. These are exploratory Protocol~v2
instruments, written after the data were seen. Section~\ref{sec:disc:mechanism}
lists the other explanations we ruled out.

\label{sec:res:quality}

secondary, structural measure. It is never tabulated beside a recovery level,

novel disruptions, and Appendix~\ref{app:rubric} gives the counts.

Read across all sixteen criteria, the rubric splits three ways. No rule for
these groups was fixed before the data were seen. We formed them by inspection
of the counts, mainly from qwen3.5 and glm-5.2, the two models with the higher
survival. The line
between parity and a small baseline lead is a judgement. Under the simple rule
``within one judgement of the baseline for both qwen3.5 and glm-5.2'', parity
would hold on eight criteria and the baseline would lead on four, since
\code{full_evacuation_kept} and the two criteria left out of the groups would
count as parity.

\begin{sloppypar}
\textbf{Parity on five criteria.} qwen3.5 and glm-5.2 reach the baseline's 10
out of 10 on \code{authority_informed} for both disruptions,
\code{reserve_committed}, \code{whole_population_planned} and
\code{stood_down_after_retraction}. The one exception is glm-5.2 on
\code{authority_informed} under conflicting orders, with 9 \texttt{yes} and
one run that could not be judged. deepseek falls behind on three of these
criteria, by one to four judgements.

\textbf{The baseline leads on five criteria.} The clearest is
\code{facility_answered_in_time}, where the baseline scores 10 and no agentic
model ever answered the facility in time. On the other four the baseline's 10
compares with qwen3.5, glm-5.2 and deepseek as follows.
\code{no_order_raises_shelter_above_beds} scores 5, 9 and 8,
\code{facility_runs_untouched} 7, 8 and 5, and
\code{in_flight_leg_lands_where_ordered} 8, 6 and 6. On
\code{full_evacuation_kept} glm-5.2 ties the baseline at 10, while qwen3.5
scores 9 and deepseek 5. We count it as a baseline lead because two of the
three models fall short.
\end{sloppypar}

\textbf{Four criteria score zero for the baseline.} Three of them indict the
scenario: \code{bus_load_within_cap},
\code{medic_sent_to_the_bus_destination} and
\code{everyone_bedded_at_the_reduced_capacity}. The baseline fails them too,
so they cannot count as agentic failures. On three of the four the agentic side
passes a few runs where the baseline passes none.
\code{shelter_set_down_within_beds} scores 2, 2 and 0 for qwen3.5, glm-5.2
and deepseek, and \code{bus_load_within_cap} scores 0, 3 and 2.

On \code{medic_sent_to_the_bus_destination} the baseline never requested
medical support, because its holding order (Section~\ref{sec:wf:fallback})
does not reach a bus already on the road. qwen3.5 and glm-5.2 met the
criterion in one run each, and deepseek in none.

\textbf{Decision quality is split.} Over the sixteen criteria, grouped by
inspection, the agentic models match the baseline on five and fall short on
five.

\textbf{Rejected messages were rare.} Over the 150 novel runs of the three
full models, the runtime dropped 35 outbound messages: 18 for deepseek, 15 for
qwen3.5 and 2 for glm-5.2 (Table~\ref{tab:app:secondary}). Twenty-one had a
message type the agent may not send, and fourteen were malformed. Every model
stayed below one rejected message per run.

The control coordinates a hundred-resident evacuation with 13 messages, all
handled by its one Incident Commander. The agentic system sends seven to eight
and a half times as many messages across its ten agents, which is nine to ten
times as many per resident delivered. It also spends roughly 800\,000 tokens
and 1.8 to 2.7 times the wall-clock that the compressed clock allows, and 140
of the 150 runs finished over budget. This budget fraction is $\phi$ of
Section~\ref{sec:eval:responsetime}.

\section{Model Sweep and Capability Threshold}
\label{sec:res:models}

The gap differs strongly between models, as agent benchmarks also find
\parencite{xu2025}. The mean novel-arm risk
difference is $-0.27$ for deepseek-v4-pro, $-0.16$ for glm-5.2 and $-0.10$ for
qwen3.5. deepseek-v4-pro has the largest deficit on all five novel
disruptions, and qwen3.5 the smallest on four of them. The exception is
\code{sc1_partial_capacity_loss}, where glm-5.2 at $-0.19$ does slightly
better than qwen3.5 at $-0.22$. The weaker models, minimax-m3 and
nemotron-3-ultra, fail badly (Table~\ref{tab:res:anticipated}). Neither is
part of the headline result.

\textbf{Hypothesis H3 cannot be resolved, because it presupposes H2.} H3
predicted that the agentic advantage on novel disruptions would be
model-robust, with a capability threshold below which that advantage
disappears. With no advantage to be robust or fragile, the threshold is
undefined. The ordering above is descriptive, and the design does not support
extrapolating it past qwen3.5.

\paragraph{Comparability of the model series.}
\label{sec:res:models:levelcap}
\label{sec:res:models:churn}
A promotion to level 4 needs a resource-efficiency judgement against the
model's own no-disruption reference, which takes three qualifying control runs.
A model without \code{agentic_closed_loop} runs is therefore capped at level 3.
qwen3.5 was at first novel-arm-only and capped by this rule, so Amendment~13
ran its ten \code{closed_loop} cells. After re-scoring it has 30 level-4 runs
and a median level of 4, against glm-5.2's 31 and 4, so levels are comparable
across the three full arms. Re-scoring changed no value of $S$ or $RD$ and no
Gate~1 verdict, because these are computed per run without the reference.
kimi-k3 (11 runs, $S = 0.773$), kimi-k2.7-code (3 runs, 0.667) and gpt-oss
(4 runs, 0.375) remain capped. They are compared on $S$ only and never ranked
by level against the full arms.

The novel arm's third model slot was held in turn by kimi-k3, gpt-oss,
kimi-k2.7-code and finally qwen3.5. Each swap was made after seeing data, so
under the pre-registration the novel arm's model set, and any analysis that
uses it, is exploratory. glm-5.2 and deepseek-v4-pro were fixed before the
novel arm ran and are unaffected.

\section{Integrity of the Reported Numbers}
\label{sec:res:integrity}

We report one measurement defect and one design limit. We do not correct the
defect in the scorer, because a correction after the fact would
rewrite a confirmatory result.

\paragraph{Six runs shelter more residents than the ceiling admits.}
\label{sec:res:integrity:ceiling}
Three qwen3.5 runs score an impossible $S = 1.1$. Three further runs, one from
qwen3.5 and two from deepseek, have the same defect but stay masked, because
their valid-resident count did not exceed the ceiling. The population caps the
feasibility ceiling at 100, while valid residents are capped only by the beds
of each shelter, 60 + 60 = 120 in total. The reported sheltered count is a sum
over logged arrival reports and has no cap at all. The scorer counts no arrival
twice, so the excess comes from the reports themselves. Each arrival
carries the headcount its driver wrote (Section~\ref{sec:eval:survival}), and
the scorer never compares it with the residents who boarded. We cap $S$ at 1.0 at the reporting layer, and no table in this
thesis shows a value above 1.0.

An over-report can only raise an agentic $S$. It cannot create the deficit,
but it can hide part of it, so the agentic figures in this chapter are, if
anything, too high. An under-report, a driver writing fewer residents than
were set down, would lower an agentic $S$. The log has no independent count of
arrivals, so we cannot rule this out.

\paragraph{One time scale, so H4 was not tested.}
\label{sec:res:h4}
Every result in this chapter uses a single \code{SIM_TIME_SCALE} of 8.4. Gate~3
therefore has one point per cell, and no crossover can be located. H4 asked at
which time scale the agentic latency cost overtakes its coordination benefit,
so H4 was not tested, and we make no crossover, trend or scale-dependence
claim.
